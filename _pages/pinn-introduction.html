A PINN is an adjustable mathematical function trained to match the information we give it, including physical equations. Let us build that idea one step at a time.

FIRST, AN ORDINARY FIT

Choose a rule. Adjust its numbers.

Suppose we measure grain size and yield strength. The Hall–Petch model supplies a particular rule:

\[\widehat\sigma_y(d)=\sigma_0+k d^{-1/2}.\]

Ordinary least squares chooses two numbers, σ₀ and k, to reduce the squared differences between predictions and measurements. The fitting procedure does not invent the Hall–Petch relation: we supplied its form and its physical interpretation.

In the accompanying notebook, 500 synthetic observations follow the ordinary branch and 10 small-grain observations follow a softening branch. Removing those ten points can make the fit look excellent while hiding another regime. A small fitting error only speaks about the data and range actually examined.

A MORE FLEXIBLE FUNCTION

What is the neural network?

Think of a curve with many adjustable knobs. A neural network combines simple operations (multiplication, addition and applying a nonlinear function) to make that curve flexible. Its weights and biases are the knobs. Together we call them θ.

\[x\ \longrightarrow\ u_\theta(x),\qquad h=\tanh(wx+b).\]

The first expression means “give the network a position; receive a predicted value.” The second shows one small building block. Evaluating the network is a forward calculation. Training is the separate process of changing its knobs to reduce an error. A flexible fitted curve does not, by itself, identify a physical mechanism.

THE PHYSICS TEST

The equation does not know the boundary values.

Consider steady diffusion in one dimension, with constant diffusivity and no sources. In dimensionless form, the field must satisfy:

\[-u''(x)=0,\quad 0<x<1,\qquad u(0)=0,\quad u(1)=1.\]

The equation says the curvature is zero. Every straight line passes that test, regardless of its slope or offset! The two boundary values select one particular line, \(u(x)=x\).

Three knobs. Two independent tests.

\[u_{a,b,c}(x)=ax+b+cx(1-x),\qquad r(x)=-u''(x)=2c.\]

Here a sets the linear slope, b shifts the field, and c adds curvature. This small trial function illustrates the loss; it is not a neural network being trained in your browser.

\[\mathcal L_f=4c^2,\qquad \mathcal L_b=\frac{b^2+(a+b-1)^2}{2}.\]

Equation only: c = 0 makes any straight line pass, even with incorrect endpoints. Boundaries only: a = 1 and b = 0 fix both endpoints, but leave c free. Both tests pass only when a = 1, b = 0 and c = 0.

A network can be designed to satisfy boundary values automatically, for example \(u_\theta=x+x(1-x)N_\theta(x)\). That is a deliberate constraint in its construction. It does not mean that the differential equation supplies the boundary values.

HOW TRAINING WORKS

Predict, check, adjust, repeat.

A real PINN replaces the three-knob curve with a network containing many weights and biases. Automatic differentiation applies the chain rule to that network expression to calculate its derivatives. It does not supply the unknown exact solution.

  1. Evaluate the network at the chosen positions.
  2. Calculate equation residuals and errors in boundary or initial values. Compare with measurements when available.
  3. Combine squared errors into a loss.
  4. Differentiate that loss with respect to the adjustable parameters, then let an optimizer update them.
\[\mathcal L=w_f\langle r_\theta^2\rangle+w_b\mathcal L_{\mathrm{boundary}}+w_i\mathcal L_{\mathrm{initial}}+w_d\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}.\]

Only include terms needed for the problem. This steady diffusion example has no initial condition and needs no interior solution labels. The weights balance numerical terms; they are not material constants. Variables and residuals should be scaled sensibly before comparing their sizes.

The notebook uses a different problem: \(-u^{\prime\prime}=\pi^2\sin(\pi x)\), with zero values at both ends. It uses tanh hidden layers, 64 training collocation points and separate coordinates for validation. It compares the final field with the known answer sin(πx). A low training residual is not enough: check unsampled points, boundary errors and the solution against an independent reference. Optimization can fail, and an incorrect equation can produce a convincingly wrong result.

See the essential PyTorch calculation
x = x.detach().clone().requires_grad_(True)
u = model(x)
ux = torch.autograd.grad(u, x, torch.ones_like(u),
                         create_graph=True)[0]
uxx = torch.autograd.grad(ux, x, torch.ones_like(ux),
                          create_graph=True)[0]
r = -uxx - torch.pi**2 * torch.sin(torch.pi*x)
loss = (r**2).mean() + w_boundary * (model(x_boundary)**2).mean()
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()

THE INVERSE QUESTION

Can we learn the diffusivity too?

Forward: given the diffusivity and the initial and boundary conditions, predict how concentration evolves. Inverse: given measured concentration profiles and suitable conditions, infer the diffusivity within an assumed model family.

\[\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\widetilde D(c;\boldsymbol\alpha)\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\right].\]

Now there are two fitted objects. The network weights θ describe a smooth concentration profile. Separate coefficients α describe the diffusivity. The diffusion equation links them. Neither object should be mistaken for the other.

A simple example of missing information: in the steady diffusion experiment above, any positive constant diffusivity gives the same straight concentration profile. The diffusivity cancels from the steady equation. A flux measurement or time-dependent data would supply information that this profile alone cannot provide.

The notebook’s staged example

  1. Choose the constitutive family. Use \(\widetilde D(c)=D_0e^{-Q(c)/(RT)}\Phi(c)\), with \(Q(c)=a_0+a_1c+a_2c^2\). Here D₀, temperature and the thermodynamic factor are supplied; three coefficients are inferred.
  2. Represent the observed profile. Locate the Matano plane and use the similarity coordinate \(\lambda=(x-x_M)/\sqrt t\). Under the diffusion-couple similarity assumptions, learn one profile \(c_\theta(\lambda)\).
  3. Fit the profile first. This gives the coupled optimization a useful starting point.
  4. Add the transformed diffusion equation. Adjust the profile network and the constitutive coefficients using profile, terminal-value and equation errors.
  5. Add three independent diffusivity measurements. Repeat from the same pretrained profile and compare the inferred diffusivity curves, not just how well the concentration fits.
The equation after the change of coordinate
\[-\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{dc}{d\lambda}=\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(\widetilde D(c)\frac{dc}{d\lambda}\right).\]

This similarity reduction assumes the appropriate diffusion-couple setting, including a composition-dependent diffusivity at fixed temperature and compatible initial and far-field conditions. The notebook also scales λ for training; derivative scale factors must be retained.

A good profile fit is not proof of a unique diffusivity.

Finite, noisy profiles and limited sampling can leave the diffusivity parameters weakly constrained. Independent diffusivity measurements provide additional information; they do not guarantee uniqueness. In an ideal scalar binary problem, an exact sufficiently resolved similarity profile and the required assumptions can support classical diffusivity reconstruction. The practical uncertainty here should not be mistaken for a claim that every binary inverse problem is fundamentally non-unique.

In the synthetic notebook the complete true curve is known for checking. An experiment does not provide that privilege. Test additional diffusion times or couples, check parameter sensitivity and uncertainty, and verify recovered parameters with an independent forward solver. Alternating points from one profile test interpolation more than independent physical prediction.

FROM A PROFILE TO DIFFUSION PHYSICS

Which diffusivity have we learned?

Our collaborative work with Prof. Aloke Paul’s Diffusion–Microstructure–Properties group at IISc connects diffusion experiments with PINN inverse modeling. Prof. Paul’s group leads the experimental diffusion studies and measurements; my group at IIT Hyderabad develops the PINN and numerical inverse-modeling methods.

Interdiffusion, intrinsic diffusion and tracer diffusion are different quantities. A concentration profile constrains the combined transport. Reproducing it does not automatically establish the individual atomic mobilities. Additional measurements must constrain the particular coefficients we want to learn.

Hemanth: the profile can agree while intrinsic diffusivities do not.

In our NiCoFeCr study, the first optimization matched concentration profiles and interdiffusion coefficients, yet the inferred intrinsic coefficients disagreed with experiment. Adding measured intrinsic diffusivities at the Kirkendall plane supplied the missing constraints. This is why checking only the fitted profile can be misleading.

WHAT CHANGES WITH AN OPERATOR?

One field, or a family of problems?

MethodWhat it representsWhat teaches it
OLSCoefficients in a chosen basisObserved input–output pairs
Ordinary NNA flexible functionUsually observed pairs
PINNUsually one solution field, coordinates → valuesEquation, conditions, optional measurements
FNOA map from input fields to solution fieldsPaired solved examples across a family

The notebook’s FNO learns forcing field → Poisson solution field. Its PINO adds an equation residual to the same operator architecture. Fourier layers alone do not make a model physics-informed. For operator-assisted inversion, freeze the trained operator and adjust its input parameters; check the recovered answer with the original solver.

Inverse design goes one step further: choose material or process settings to achieve a desired performance. That is a different question from identifying diffusivity. It can use surrogates and optimization, but it is not automatically a PINN.

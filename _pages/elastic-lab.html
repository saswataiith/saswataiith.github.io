Physics: eigenstrain, Eshelby’s inclusion and the habit line

Eigenstrain

Eigenstrain is the stress-free transformation strain \(\epsilon^0\). When a region transforms with no external constraint, its stress stays zero:

\[\sigma=C:(\epsilon-\epsilon^0)=0,\]

where \(C\) is the elastic stiffness tensor and \(\epsilon\) the total strain. In the free region the total strain equals the transformation strain, \(\epsilon=\epsilon^0\). Examples are the lattice-misfit dilatation of a precipitate, \(\epsilon^0_{ij}=\delta_{ij}(a_p-a_m)/a_m\), thermal expansion, and the spontaneous distortion of a lattice during a phase change. In this module \(\epsilon^0=\epsilon_\eta\,\mathrm{diag}(1,t)\) in the crystal axes: principal strains \(\epsilon_1=\epsilon_\eta\) along [10] and \(\epsilon_2=t\,\epsilon_\eta\) along [01].

Eshelby’s four steps

To find the stress when the transforming region is constrained by a surrounding elastic matrix, Eshelby (1957) used a thought experiment:

  1. Cut the region Ω (the inclusion) out of the matrix and let it undergo its stress-free transformation \(\epsilon^0\).
  2. Apply surface tractions to force it back into its original hole. This imposes elastic strain \(-\epsilon^0\), i.e. stress \(-C:\epsilon^0\).
  3. Weld the interface back together.
  4. Release the tractions. The inclusion and the matrix relax together to mechanical equilibrium.

The relaxation leaves a constrained total strain \(\epsilon^c\) inside the inclusion. The elastic strain is the difference between the constrained and the stress-free geometry, and only it stores energy:

\[e=\epsilon^c-\epsilon^0,\qquad \sigma=C:e,\qquad w=\tfrac12\,e:C:e\ \ \text{(energy per unit volume)} .\]

For an ellipsoidal inclusion (an ellipse in 2-D) in a homogeneous infinite matrix, Eshelby proved that \(\epsilon^c\) is uniform and linear in the eigenstrain, \(\epsilon^c=S:\epsilon^0\). The Eshelby tensor S depends on the inclusion’s shape and orientation and on the elastic constants. This is where orientation dependence comes from: the same \(\epsilon^0\) costs different energy in differently oriented inclusions.

Thin-plate limit: a negotiation at the interface

Eshelby’s theory is a negotiation of space. The transformed region wants a new shape; the surrounding matrix resists. The theory decides who yields, and where stress is left trapped. For a very thin plate (a martensite lath, a plate-shaped precipitate) the whole negotiation happens at the flat interface. The plate is long compared with its thickness, so the matrix, which extends far beyond the plate and is unstrained away from its ends, dictates what happens along the plate. Across its thickness, the plate is free to relax.

Why jump conditions appear at an inclusion

The eigenstrain switches on abruptly at the interface: it is zero in the matrix and \(\epsilon^0\) in the plate. Suppose every strain and stress component were continuous across the interface. Hooke’s law, \(\epsilon^0=\epsilon-C^{-1}:\sigma\), would then make the eigenstrain continuous as well. It is not. So some components of strain or stress must jump. Two physical laws decide which ones:

  • No tearing, no overlapping. The interface is welded, so the displacement is continuous. This constrains the strain.
  • Newton’s third law. The force that the plate exerts on the matrix across the interface equals and opposes the force the matrix exerts on the plate. This constrains the stress.

The interface handshake

1. The matrix rules the length (Hadamard kinematics). Plate and matrix cannot separate, so their displacements match everywhere along the interface direction \(\mathbf s\). Their derivatives along \(\mathbf s\) then match too, and the plate’s total strain along the interface equals the matrix value: \(\epsilon_{ss}=0\). The plate is still free to thicken, thin or shear across its thickness (direction \(\mathbf m\)). That freedom is a jump in the displacement gradient of the form \(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m\), where \(\mathbf b\) is not yet known.

2. The forces rule the thickness (traction continuity). The forces pushing, pulling and sliding across the interface must balance. So the traction vector \(\sigma\mathbf m\), which contains the normal stress \(\sigma_{mm}\) and the shear stress \(\sigma_{sm}\), is continuous. No balance law acts on the stress that stretches the plate along its length, \(\sigma_{ss}\): it may differ completely from the matrix value.

Across the welded interface, each component is fixed by exactly one law. When the strain component is fixed, the matching stress component may jump, and the other way round:

componentstrain \(\epsilon\)stress \(\sigma\)
ss (along the plate)continuous: Hadamard (matches the matrix)free to jump: remnant stress
sm (shear)free to jump: set by \(\mathbf b\)continuous: traction
mm (across the plate)free to jump: set by \(\mathbf b\)continuous: traction

Counting. In 2-D a symmetric strain has 3 components. Hadamard fixes 1 of them (\(\epsilon_{ss}\)); the other 2 are carried by the 2 unknown components of \(\mathbf b\). Traction continuity supplies 2 equations (\(\sigma_{sm},\sigma_{mm}\)), exactly enough to find \(\mathbf b\). In 3-D: 6 strain components, 3 fixed in-plane by Hadamard, 3 carried by \(\mathbf b\), and 3 traction equations to find them.

Duality and the remnant stress

The unknown deformation \(\mathbf b\) and the interface traction \(\sigma\mathbf m\) are work conjugates: a movement and the force that resists it. Changing \(\mathbf b\) by \(\delta\mathbf b\) changes the plate’s energy density by \(\delta w=(\sigma\mathbf m)\cdot\delta\mathbf b\). So when the plate chooses \(\mathbf b\) to minimise its energy, the traction on its faces is driven to zero: \(\sigma_{mm}=\sigma_{sm}=0\). The plate relaxes completely across its thickness.

What is left is the stress along the plate. The plate is still held to zero length change along \(\mathbf s\), although it wanted to change by \(\epsilon^0_{ss}\). It carries the elastic strain \(e_{ss}=-\epsilon^0_{ss}\) and, because its faces are free, a single uniaxial stress

\[\sigma_{ss}=-E_s\,\epsilon^0_{ss},\qquad w=\tfrac12E_s\left(\epsilon^0_{ss}\right)^2=\tfrac12B(\mathbf m),\]

where \(E_s\) is the (plane-strain) stiffness of the crystal along \(\mathbf s\). This is the whole elastic penalty of a thin plate: the remnant stress it pays for staying compatible with the matrix. It vanishes only when \(\epsilon^0_{ss}=0\), on the habit line. In the worked case of Step 7(vii) the plate wanted to shrink by 0.005 along \(\mathbf s\), was held at its original length, and carries a tension of 0.0133. Figure 3 shows every component across that interface.

Names

  • \(\mathbf n,\ \mathbf q\): an arbitrary pair of orthogonal unit vectors defining a rotated frame; \(\mathbf n\) is at angle θ from [10].
  • \(\mathbf s\): the invariant (habit) line direction, the value of \(\mathbf n\) where \(\epsilon^0_{nn}=0\). Green line on the plot.
  • \(\mathbf m\): the habit normal, orthogonal to \(\mathbf s\); the value of \(\mathbf q\) at that θ. Orange arrow on the plot.

“Habit angle” is used in the literature for both the line and the normal, which differ by 90°. This page always says which.

Limits

The ideal plate theory assumes an infinitely long plate, a sharp interface, a uniform transformation strain, linear small-strain elasticity and a homogeneous stiffness. Real plates have ends, finite thickness, interfacial energy and diffuse interfaces, and plasticity can destroy coherency. In this laboratory, Module II adds elastic anisotropy, Module III two phases with different eigenstrains, and Module IV plates stiffer or softer than the matrix.

Planned extensions. Future modules will relax the remaining assumptions: finite aspect ratios through the full Eshelby tensor, interfacial energy and phase-field gradients for diffuse interfaces and evolving shapes, dislocation arrays for the loss of coherency, and the 3-D invariant-plane analysis of the phenomenological theory of martensite crystallography.

Mathematics: derivation, step by step

Small strain; crystal axes x = [10], y = [01]; repeated indices summed. Vectors are columns.

Step 1. Rotate \(\epsilon^0\) into the \((\mathbf n,\mathbf q)\) frame.

In its principal frame the eigenstrain is diagonal, \(\epsilon^0=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1&0\\0&\epsilon_2\end{pmatrix}\). A frame at angle θ has basis vectors and rotation matrix (rows = new basis vectors)

\[\mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\ \sin\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf q=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\\ \cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.\]

Each new component is the tensor sandwiched between two basis vectors, e.g. \(\epsilon^0_{nq}=\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\epsilon^0\,\mathbf q=n_i\,\epsilon^0_{ij}\,q_j\). All four at once are \(\epsilon'=R\,\epsilon^0R^{\mathsf T}\). Multiply in full:

\[R\,\epsilon^0=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1\cos\theta&\epsilon_2\sin\theta\\-\epsilon_1\sin\theta&\epsilon_2\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\] \[R\,\epsilon^0R^{\mathsf T} =\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1\cos\theta&\epsilon_2\sin\theta\\-\epsilon_1\sin\theta&\epsilon_2\cos\theta\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1\cos^2\theta+\epsilon_2\sin^2\theta&(\epsilon_2-\epsilon_1)\sin\theta\cos\theta\\ (\epsilon_2-\epsilon_1)\sin\theta\cos\theta&\epsilon_1\sin^2\theta+\epsilon_2\cos^2\theta\end{pmatrix}.\]

So the entries are

\[\epsilon^0_{nn}=\epsilon_1\cos^2\theta+\epsilon_2\sin^2\theta,\qquad \epsilon^0_{qq}=\epsilon_1\sin^2\theta+\epsilon_2\cos^2\theta,\qquad \epsilon^0_{nq}=(\epsilon_2-\epsilon_1)\sin\theta\cos\theta .\]

Check: the trace \(\epsilon^0_{nn}+\epsilon^0_{qq}=\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2\) and the determinant \(\epsilon^0_{nn}\epsilon^0_{qq}-(\epsilon^0_{nq})^2=\epsilon_1\epsilon_2\) do not change with θ. For this module put \(\epsilon_1=\epsilon_\eta\), \(\epsilon_2=t\,\epsilon_\eta\): \(\epsilon^0_{nn}=\epsilon_\eta(\cos^2\theta+t\sin^2\theta)\), the curve in the “Normal eigenstrain along n(θ)” plot. At a habit root, \(\mathbf n\) becomes \(\mathbf s\) and \(\mathbf q\) becomes \(\mathbf m\).

Step 2. Eshelby’s thin plate.

(a) Hadamard compatibility. Let the interface be the plane with normal \(\mathbf m\). The displacement \(\mathbf u\) is continuous across it, so its derivative along any direction \(\mathbf s\) lying in the interface (\(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf m=0\)) is the same on both sides. The jump in the gradient therefore kills every in-plane vector:

\[[\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]\,\mathbf s=\mathbf 0\quad\text{for all }\mathbf s\perp\mathbf m \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad [\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]=\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m,\qquad \mathbf b=[\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]\,\mathbf m .\]

The matrix far from the plate ends is unstrained, so inside the plate \(\nabla\mathbf u=\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m\). Here \(\mathbf b\) is an unknown vector (a displacement per unit distance across the plate). In index notation,

\[\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}=b_i\,m_j ,\]

where i labels the displacement component and j the direction of the derivative. In 3-D, with \(\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)^{\mathsf T}\), \(\mathbf b=(b_1,b_2,b_3)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\mathbf m=(m_1,m_2,m_3)^{\mathsf T}\), the outer product is the column \(\mathbf b\) times the row \(\mathbf m^{\mathsf T}\):

\[\nabla\mathbf u= \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_1}&\dfrac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_2}&\dfrac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_3}\\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_1}&\dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_2}&\dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_3}\\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_1}&\dfrac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_2}&\dfrac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_3} \end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\\b_3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m_1&m_2&m_3\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} b_1m_1&b_1m_2&b_1m_3\\ b_2m_1&b_2m_2&b_2m_3\\ b_3m_1&b_3m_2&b_3m_3 \end{pmatrix}.\]

Every entry of the gradient is fixed by one component of \(\mathbf b\) and one of \(\mathbf m\); for example the shear gradient \(\partial u_1/\partial x_2=b_1m_2\). The matrix has rank one: every row is a multiple of \(\mathbf m^{\mathsf T}\), so \(\nabla\mathbf u\,\mathbf s=\mathbf b\,(\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf s)=\mathbf 0\) for any in-plane \(\mathbf s\). Nothing changes along the interface. In this 2-D laboratory \(m_3=b_3=0\) and the upper-left block remains:

\[\nabla\mathbf u=\begin{pmatrix}b_1m_1&b_1m_2\\b_2m_1&b_2m_2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \epsilon^c=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)=\tfrac12(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m+\mathbf m\otimes\mathbf b) =\begin{pmatrix}b_1m_1&\tfrac12(b_1m_2+b_2m_1)\\\tfrac12(b_1m_2+b_2m_1)&b_2m_2\end{pmatrix}.\]

(b) Energy. The elastic strain is \(e=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)-\epsilon^0\). With \(\sigma^0=C:\epsilon^0\) and the acoustic tensor \(Q_{ik}(\mathbf m)=C_{ijkl}m_jm_l\), and using the symmetries of C,

\[w(\mathbf b)=\tfrac12\,e:C:e =\tfrac12\Big[\epsilon^0:C:\epsilon^0-2\,\mathbf b\cdot(\sigma^0\mathbf m)+\mathbf b\cdot Q(\mathbf m)\,\mathbf b\Big].\]

(c) Traction balance. Minimising over \(\mathbf b\), \(\partial w/\partial\mathbf b=Q(\mathbf m)\mathbf b-\sigma^0\mathbf m=\mathbf 0\). This is exactly zero traction on the faces, \(\sigma\mathbf m=(C:e)\,\mathbf m=\mathbf 0\). Substituting \(\mathbf b=Q(\mathbf m)^{-1}\sigma^0\mathbf m\) back:

\[\boxed{\,w=\tfrac12\Big[\epsilon^0:C:\epsilon^0-(\sigma^0\mathbf m)\cdot Q(\mathbf m)^{-1}(\sigma^0\mathbf m)\Big]=\tfrac12\,B(\mathbf m)\,}\]

The first term is the cost of forcing the plate fully back (Eshelby’s step 2). The second is what it recovers by relaxing across its thickness (step 4). Their difference is the elastic energy kernel \(B(\mathbf m)\) of Module II and of Appendix A. In Eshelby’s language this is the thin-plate limit, with \(S:\epsilon^0=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)\).

(d) The two jump conditions, derived. Let \([\![f]\!]=f^{\rm plate}-f^{\rm matrix}\) denote a jump across the interface. Kinematic: \(\mathbf u\) is continuous, so for any in-plane \(\mathbf s\) the directional derivative is continuous, \([\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]\mathbf s=\mathbf 0\), which gives \([\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]=\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m\) as in (a). Static: apply \(\int_{\partial P}\sigma\,\mathbf n\,dA=\mathbf 0\) to a pillbox P of face area A straddling the interface. As its height goes to zero the side faces vanish and only the two flat faces remain:

\[(\sigma^{\rm plate}-\sigma^{\rm matrix})\,\mathbf m\,A=\mathbf 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [\![\sigma]\!]\,\mathbf m=\mathbf 0 .\]

With a homogeneous C, \([\![\sigma]\!]=C:\big(\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)-[\![\epsilon^0]\!]\big)\), so the static condition reads \(Q(\mathbf m)\,\mathbf b=(C:[\![\epsilon^0]\!])\,\mathbf m\). These hold at every point of every coherent interface. For the thin plate the matrix side is unstrained and \([\![\epsilon^0]\!]=\epsilon^0\), which returns \(Q(\mathbf m)\mathbf b=\sigma^0\mathbf m\) of (c): the thin-plate solution is the pair of jump conditions.

(e) Duality. Vary \(\mathbf b\): \(\delta w=\sigma:\delta e=\sigma:\operatorname{sym}(\delta\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m) =\delta b_i\,\sigma_{ij}m_j=(\sigma\mathbf m)\cdot\delta\mathbf b\). The traction is the work conjugate of \(\mathbf b\), so \(\partial w/\partial\mathbf b=\mathbf 0\) and \(\sigma\mathbf m=\mathbf 0\) are the same statement.

(f) The remnant stress and a one-line energy. With \(\sigma\mathbf m=\mathbf 0\), the only stress left in the \((\mathbf s,\mathbf m)\) frame is \(\sigma_{ss}\): \(\sigma=\sigma_{ss}\,\mathbf s\otimes\mathbf s\). Hadamard fixes \(\epsilon_{ss}=0\), so \(e_{ss}=-\epsilon^0_{ss}\). Using the compliance \(S=C^{-1}\) (plane strain), \(e_{ss}=S_{ssss}\,\sigma_{ss}\), hence

\[\sigma_{ss}=-\frac{\epsilon^0_{ss}}{S_{ssss}}=-E_s\,\epsilon^0_{ss},\qquad w=\tfrac12\,\sigma_{ss}\,e_{ss}=\tfrac12\,E_s\left(\epsilon^0_{ss}\right)^2,\qquad \boxed{\,B(\mathbf m)=E_s\left(\epsilon^0_{ss}\right)^2\,}.\]

This is exact for any homogeneous stiffness, isotropic or anisotropic, and it makes Step 3 immediate. For the isotropic law of this page \(E_s=(C_{11}^2-C_{12}^2)/C_{11}=8/3\) in every direction, so \(B=\tfrac83(0.005)^2=6.67\times10^{-5}\) for a plate along [01] and \(\tfrac83(0.01)^2=2.67\times10^{-4}\) along [10], matching Step 7 and the Module II curve. In an anisotropic crystal \(E_s\) depends on the direction, which is how elastic anisotropy enters in Module II.

Step 3. \(B(\mathbf m)=0\iff\epsilon^0_{ss}=0\).

(⇒) If \(B(\mathbf m)=0\) then \(w=0\), and because C is positive definite, \(e=0\): the total strain equals the transformation strain, \(\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)=\epsilon^0\). Project along the interface direction \(\mathbf s\) (\(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf m=0\)):

\[\epsilon^0_{ss}=\mathbf s\cdot\Big[\tfrac12(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m+\mathbf m\otimes\mathbf b)\Big]\mathbf s =\tfrac12\big[(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf b)(\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf s)+(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf m)(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf s)\big]=0 .\]

(⇐) If \(\epsilon^0_{ss}=0\), write both sides in the \((\mathbf s,\mathbf m)\) frame, where \(\mathbf m=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}\):

\[\epsilon'=\begin{pmatrix}0&\epsilon^0_{sm}\\\epsilon^0_{sm}&\epsilon^0_{mm}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \operatorname{sym}(\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf m)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\tfrac12a_s\\\tfrac12a_s&a_m\end{pmatrix}.\]

They match with \(a_s=2\epsilon^0_{sm}\), \(a_m=\epsilon^0_{mm}\). Then \(\mathbf b=\mathbf a\) gives \(e=0\) and \(B(\mathbf m)=0\). The condition is kinematic: it holds for every stiffness C. The elastic constants only decide how costly the other orientations are.

Step 4. Solve for θ and the plate kinematics.

With \(\epsilon_1=\epsilon_\eta\), \(\epsilon_2=t\,\epsilon_\eta\) and \(\cos\theta\ne0\):

\[\epsilon^0_{ss}=\epsilon_\eta\left(\cos^2\theta+t\sin^2\theta\right)=0 \iff\tan^2\theta=-\frac1t \iff\theta_1=\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{-t}},\quad\theta_2=180^\circ-\theta_1 .\]

A real root requires \(t\le0\) (\(t=0\) is the limit θ = 90°). On the root \(\cos^2\theta=-t/(1-t)\), \(\sin^2\theta=1/(1-t)\) and \(\sin\theta\cos\theta=\pm\sqrt{-t}/(1-t)\). Substituting into Step 1:

\[\epsilon^0_{mm}=\epsilon_\eta(1+t)\ \ \text{(trace invariance, since }\epsilon^0_{ss}=0),\qquad \epsilon^0_{sm}=(t-1)\,\epsilon_\eta\sin\theta\cos\theta=\mp\,\epsilon_\eta\sqrt{-t},\] \[\gamma=2\epsilon^0_{sm}=\mp\,2\epsilon_\eta\sqrt{-t}\ \ \text{(engineering shear)},\qquad \omega=\epsilon^0_{sm}\ \ \text{(rigid lattice rotation, radians)}.\]

The upper sign is for \(\theta_1\), the lower for \(\theta_2\). The rotation is the antisymmetric part of \(\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf m\): \(\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf m=\epsilon^0+\epsilon^0_{sm}(\mathbf s\otimes\mathbf m-\mathbf m\otimes\mathbf s)\). In units of \(\epsilon_\eta\):

Step 5. Where \(\alpha=\tan^{-1}\sqrt{-t}\) comes from.

The habit condition can be written for the line or for the normal. Both routes give the same plate.

Route A: start from the line. Step 4 gave the line angle through \(\tan\theta_1=1/\sqrt{-t}\). The normal is the line turned by 90°:

\[\mathbf s=(\cos\theta_1,\ \sin\theta_1),\qquad \mathbf m=(-\sin\theta_1,\ \cos\theta_1).\]

The acute angle α between the normal and [10] satisfies \(\tan\alpha=|m_y/m_x|=\cos\theta_1/\sin\theta_1=1/\tan\theta_1\). So

\[\tan\alpha=\frac{1}{\tan\theta_1}=\frac{1}{1/\sqrt{-t}}=\sqrt{-t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha=\tan^{-1}\sqrt{-t}=90^\circ-\theta_1 .\]

Route B: start from the normal. Call the normal angle α, so \(\mathbf m=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)\). The tangent is \(\mathbf s=(-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)\). Put \(\mathbf s\) into the stretch formula of Step 1 (the same sandwich \(\mathbf s\cdot\epsilon^0\mathbf s\)):

\[\epsilon^0_{ss}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix} \epsilon_\eta\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&t\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}-\sin\alpha\\ \cos\alpha\end{pmatrix} =\epsilon_\eta\left(\sin^2\alpha+t\cos^2\alpha\right).\]

Set it to zero and divide by \(\cos^2\alpha\):

\[\sin^2\alpha+t\cos^2\alpha=0\iff\tan^2\alpha=-t\iff \alpha=\pm\tan^{-1}\sqrt{-t}.\]

Route B is the form you meet when the energy is written in terms of the normal, as in Module II and in the thesis. The same bookkeeping, line versus normal, carries over to habit planes in 3-D, a planned extension of this laboratory. Step 6. Mohr’s circle.

Apply \(\cos^2\theta=\tfrac12(1+\cos2\theta)\), \(\sin^2\theta=\tfrac12(1-\cos2\theta)\) and \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta\) to the Step 1 entries:

\[\epsilon^0_{nn}=\bar c+R\cos2\theta,\qquad \epsilon^0_{nq}=-R\sin2\theta,\qquad \bar c=\epsilon_\eta\frac{1+t}{2},\quad R=\epsilon_\eta\frac{1-t}{2}.\]

As θ grows, \((\epsilon^0_{nn},\epsilon^0_{nq})\) moves round a circle with centre \(\bar c\) and radius R, turning by −2θ. The vertical axis is tensor shear; engineering shear is twice it. The circle meets \(\epsilon^0_{nn}=0\) only if \(R\ge\bar c\), i.e. \(t\le0\), at

\[\epsilon^0_{nq}=\pm\sqrt{R^2-\bar c^2}=\pm\,\epsilon_\eta\sqrt{-t},\]

the green dots: the same shear \(\epsilon^0_{sm}\) as in Step 4. (The plot labels the centre c; it is \(\bar c\) here to avoid clashing with \(\cos\theta\).)

Step 7. One case solved completely.

Take the page defaults: \(t=-0.5\), \(\epsilon_\eta=0.01\), and the isotropic stiffness of Module II, \(C_{11}=3\), \(C_{12}=1\), \(C_{44}=1\) (dimensionless). We find the habit line, then compute the plate energy twice: at the habit normal and at a poor orientation.

(i) Eigenstrain.

\[\epsilon^0=\begin{pmatrix}0.01&0\\0&-0.005\end{pmatrix}.\]

(ii) Habit line. \(\tan^2\theta=-1/t=2\), so \(\tan\theta_1=\sqrt2\), \(\theta_1=54.7356^\circ\). Then \(\cos^2\theta_1=1/3\), \(\sin^2\theta_1=2/3\):

\[\cos\theta_1=0.57735,\qquad\sin\theta_1=0.81650,\qquad R=\begin{pmatrix}0.57735&0.81650\\-0.81650&0.57735\end{pmatrix}.\]

(iii) Rotate the eigenstrain (Step 1 with numbers).

\[R\,\epsilon^0=\begin{pmatrix}0.0057735&-0.0040825\\-0.0081650&-0.0028868\end{pmatrix},\qquad \epsilon'=R\,\epsilon^0R^{\mathsf T}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-0.0070711\\-0.0070711&0.005\end{pmatrix}.\]

Entry by entry: \(\epsilon^0_{ss}=0.01\cdot\tfrac13-0.005\cdot\tfrac23=0\); \(\epsilon^0_{mm}=0.01\cdot\tfrac23-0.005\cdot\tfrac13=0.005\); \(\epsilon^0_{sm}=-0.015\times0.57735\times0.81650=-0.0070711\). Trace 0.005 and determinant \(-5\times10^{-5}\) agree with the original diagonal matrix. The Mohr readout shows the last entry as \(-0.7071\,\epsilon_\eta\).

(iv) The frame vectors and the compatible vector a.

\[\mathbf s=\begin{pmatrix}0.57735\\0.81650\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf m=\begin{pmatrix}-0.81650\\0.57735\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf a=2\epsilon^0_{sm}\mathbf s+\epsilon^0_{mm}\mathbf m =\begin{pmatrix}-0.012247\\-0.0086603\end{pmatrix}.\]

Check that it reproduces the eigenstrain in crystal axes:

\[\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf m=\begin{pmatrix}a_xm_x&a_xm_y\\a_ym_x&a_ym_y\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0.01&-0.0070711\\0.0070711&-0.005\end{pmatrix},\] \[\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf m)=\begin{pmatrix}0.01&0\\0&-0.005\end{pmatrix}=\epsilon^0,\qquad \operatorname{skew}(\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf m)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-0.0070711\\0.0070711&0\end{pmatrix}.\]

The skew part is a rotation by 0.0070711 rad (0.41°). The eigenstrain is exactly a compatible plate deformation plus this rigid rotation.

(v) Stiffness in matrix form. For the in-plane cubic law with tensor shear, \(\sigma_{xx}=C_{11}\epsilon_{xx}+C_{12}\epsilon_{yy}\), \(\sigma_{yy}=C_{12}\epsilon_{xx}+C_{11}\epsilon_{yy}\), \(\sigma_{xy}=2C_{44}\epsilon_{xy}\). The eigenstress is

\[\sigma^0=C:\epsilon^0=\begin{pmatrix}3(0.01)+1(-0.005)&0\\0&1(0.01)+3(-0.005)\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0.025&0\\0&-0.005\end{pmatrix},\qquad \epsilon^0:C:\epsilon^0=\sigma^0:\epsilon^0=2.75\times10^{-4}.\]

The acoustic tensor \(Q_{ik}(\mathbf m)=C_{ijkl}m_jm_l\) for this law is

\[Q(\mathbf m)=\begin{pmatrix}C_{11}m_x^2+C_{44}m_y^2&(C_{12}+C_{44})m_xm_y\\ (C_{12}+C_{44})m_xm_y&C_{44}m_x^2+C_{11}m_y^2\end{pmatrix}.\]

(vi) Energy at the habit normal. With \(m_x^2=2/3\), \(m_y^2=1/3\), \(m_xm_y=-0.47140\):

\[Q(\mathbf m)=\begin{pmatrix}2.3333&-0.94281\\-0.94281&1.6667\end{pmatrix},\quad \det Q=3,\quad Q^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}1.6667&0.94281\\0.94281&2.3333\end{pmatrix},\] \[\sigma^0\mathbf m=\begin{pmatrix}0.025(-0.81650)\\-0.005(0.57735)\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}-0.020412\\-0.0028868\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf b=Q^{-1}\sigma^0\mathbf m=\begin{pmatrix}-0.012247\\-0.0086603\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf a .\] \[(\sigma^0\mathbf m)\cdot\mathbf b=2.50\times10^{-4}+0.25\times10^{-4}=2.75\times10^{-4} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B(\mathbf m)=2.75\times10^{-4}-2.75\times10^{-4}=0 .\]

The plate relaxes all of its eigenstrain: \(\mathbf b=\mathbf a\), \(e=0\), no stress, no energy.

(vii) Energy at a poor orientation. Put the plate along [01], so \(\mathbf m=(1,0)\):

\[Q=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma^0\mathbf m=\begin{pmatrix}0.025\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf b=\begin{pmatrix}0.0083333\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad B=2.75\times10^{-4}-0.025\times0.0083333=6.67\times10^{-5}.\] \[e=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)-\epsilon^0 =\begin{pmatrix}-0.0016667&0\\0&0.005\end{pmatrix},\qquad \sigma=C:e=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0.013333\end{pmatrix}.\]

Read this physically. \(\sigma_{xx}=0\): the faces (normal x) carry no traction, as Step 2 demands. \(e_{yy}=0.005\): along the plate the particle wanted to shrink by 0.005 but the matrix holds its length, so it is stretched elastically and carries tension 0.0133. The energy density is \(\tfrac12 e:\sigma=\tfrac12(0.005)(0.013333)=3.33\times10^{-5} =\tfrac12B\). Module II’s curve gives \(B=6.67\times10^{-5}\) at normal 0° and \(2.67\times10^{-4}\) at 90°, and zero at the habit normals.

References. J. D. Eshelby, Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A 241, 376–396 (1957). T. Mura, Micromechanics of Defects in Solids, 2nd ed., Kluwer (1987). A. G. Khachaturyan, Theory of Structural Transformations in Solids, Wiley (1983).

MODULE II

The stiffness is not the energy.

Keep the eigenstrain from Module I and change the elastic constants. The plots calculate the elastic energy kernel \(B_{pq}(\mathbf n)\), not Young’s modulus. The polar angle here denotes a modulation normal, not a plate tangent.

Homogeneous stiffness

Why did this happen?

Physics

Eigenstrain specifies what the phase wants to do. Stiffness specifies the cost of preventing it. Mechanical relaxation reduces that cost. Their combination gives B(n). A minimum identifies a low-penalty modulation normal; the corresponding ideal plate is perpendicular to it. Several equal minima are symmetry-related choices.

Mathematics

From my Appendix A, Eqs. (A.1–A.12), pp. 107–111. This is the elastic-energy derivation developed using Khachaturyan’s microelasticity framework. Spatial indices \(i,j,k,l\) are summed; phase indices \(p,q\) are summed only where shown. The laboratory uses two spatial dimensions.

1. Separate eigenstrain, mean strain and periodic relaxation.

\[\epsilon^0_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\sum_p\theta_p(\mathbf r)\epsilon^{(p)}_{ij},\qquad e_{ij}=E_{ij}+\tfrac12(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i})-\epsilon^0_{ij}.\]\[F_e=\tfrac12\int_V C_{ijkl}e_{ij}e_{kl}\,dV.\]

For homogeneous stiffness and zero applied mean stress, \(E_{ij}=\langle\epsilon^0_{ij}\rangle\). The mean terms cancel in the six-integral expansion of Appendix A. Only the fluctuations \(\Delta\theta_p=\theta_p-\langle\theta_p\rangle\) remain.

2. Solve equilibrium for each nonzero Fourier mode.

\[\widehat\sigma^{(p)}_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\epsilon^{(p)}_{kl},\quad Q_{ik}(\mathbf n)=C_{ijkl}n_jn_l,\quad\Omega_{ik}=(Q^{-1})_{ik}.\]\[\widetilde u_k=-\frac{\mathrm i}{|\mathbf k|^2}\Omega_{ki}(\mathbf n)k_j\sum_p\widehat\sigma^{(p)}_{ij}\widetilde\theta_p,\qquad\mathbf n=\mathbf k/|\mathbf k|.\]

The acoustic contraction leaves \(i,k\) free. It is not the contraction \(C_{ijkl}n_kn_l\), which leaves a different index pair free.

3. Substitute the relaxed displacement into the energy.

\[\boxed{B_{pq}(\mathbf n)=C_{ijkl}\epsilon^{(p)}_{ij}\epsilon^{(q)}_{kl}-n_j\widehat\sigma^{(p)}_{ij}\Omega_{ki}(\mathbf n)\widehat\sigma^{(q)}_{kl}n_l}\]\[F_e=\frac12\sum_{p,q}\int_{\mathbf k\ne0} B_{pq}(\mathbf n)\widetilde\theta_p(\mathbf k)\widetilde\theta_q^*(\mathbf k)\frac{d^d k}{(2\pi)^d}.\]

\(B_{pq}\) is the elastic energy kernel. Its first term is the unrelaxed eigenstrain contraction; the second is the energy reduction from mechanical relaxation. It becomes a total energy only after weighting by the field spectra and summing or integrating over modes. This is Eq. (A.12), with \(d=2\) here.

4. Differentiate the energy: Appendix B, Eqs. (B.1–B.12).

\[g_a=\frac{\delta F_e}{\delta\theta_a},\qquad \widetilde g_a=\sum_q B_{aq}\widetilde\theta_q.\]

Here \(a\) is a fixed field label, not a summed spatial index. The two equal variations of the quadratic form cancel its factor of one half. We use \(B_{aq}=B_{qa}\), renaming dummy indices explicitly. For \(\theta_a=\eta_a^2\), the chain rule gives \(\delta F_e/\delta\eta_a=2\eta_a g_a\) in real space.

Boundary-condition distinction: Appendix A uses a relaxed mean strain. The finite-plate module fixes \(E_{ij}=0\) and therefore retains the additional mean energy \(\tfrac{V}{2}(E-\langle\epsilon^0\rangle):C:(E-\langle\epsilon^0\rangle)\). Its zero mode must not be silently dropped.

\(A_Z=2C_{44}/(C_{11}-C_{12})\). The 2-D law uses the in-plane cubic sub-block, with tensor shear \(\sigma_{xy}=2C_{44}e_{xy}\), no out-of-plane eigenstrain, and no plane-stress condensation.

MODULE III

Two phases, three kernel terms.

β and γ sit in an α matrix with the homogeneous stiffness from Module II. Each phase can have a different eigenstrain. Compare the self terms and cross term before looking at a particle pair.

Why did this happen?

Now calculate a particle-pair energy.

Why did this happen?

Physics

Changing one misfit sign reverses the cross coupling because it is bilinear in the two eigenstrains. Both self terms remain nonnegative. However, Bβγ(n) is a reciprocal-space coefficient. To obtain real-space interaction energy at separation R, we must also include particle shape spectra and Fourier phases. Single-particle energy and pair-interaction energy are different quantities.

Mathematics
\[E_{\rm int}(\mathbf R)=E_{\beta+\gamma}-E_\beta-E_\gamma\]\[=\frac1{N^4}\sum_{\mathbf k}B_{\beta\gamma}(\hat{\mathbf k})|\hat h(\mathbf k)|^2\cos(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R).\]

The k = 0 coefficient in this clamped periodic calculation is εβ:C:εγ. The square contains identical circular Gaussian profiles h, with width 0.045 of the box. Each Fourier mode contributes; the kernel is not simply evaluated at the separation angle.

Computation

Use a 64 × 64 periodic square and exact Fourier translations of the sampled Gaussian. Evaluate a 1° separation-angle sweep. Periodic copies are included. The profiles have diffuse tails. The on-screen circles indicate their positions, not sharp boundaries.

(3.27–3.29) give C11 = 7000, C12 = 5000, C44 = 3000. The validated self values are 0.342857 on [10]/[01] and 0.8 on diagonals; cross values are −0.342857 and −0.8. Why did this happen?

Physics

Soft and hard refer to the elastic response relative to the matrix. The same eigenstrain is accommodated differently when stiffness varies in space. With isotropic phases and dilatational misfit, an infinite medium has no preferred material direction. Small angular differences in this periodic calculation require particular care: the square cell and finite grid also introduce directions. We fix the plate shape to isolate its orientation energy, rather than claim to predict its unconstrained equilibrium morphology.

Physical insight: A minimum is the smallest elastic penalty among the sampled orientations of this prescribed plate, under the stated boundary condition. It is not automatically a habit plane or a stress-free state.

Mathematics

Inhomogeneous equilibrium: Bhattacharyya, Heo, Chang and Chen, Communications in Computational Physics 11 (2012), 726–738, Eqs. (2.6–2.16). Tushar Jogi’s thesis, §§3.1.4–3.1.6 and Appendix B, supplies the related phase-field formulation and driving-force derivation. Indices are retained here so each contraction can be checked.

\[C_{ijkl}(\mathbf r)=C^{\rm ref}_{ijkl}+\Delta C_{ijkl}(\mathbf r),\quad e_{ij}=E_{ij}+\tfrac12(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i})-\epsilon^0_{ij}.\]\[\partial_j(C_{ijkl}e_{kl})=0.\]

The spectral iteration, with every index visible.

\[C^{\rm ref}_{ijkl}u_{k,lj}=\partial_j P_{ij},\qquad P_{ij}=C_{ijkl}(\epsilon^0_{kl}-E_{kl})-\Delta C_{ijkl}u_{k,l}.\]\[Q^{\rm ref}_{ik}=C^{\rm ref}_{ijkl}n_jn_l,\qquad\widetilde u_k^{(r+1)}=-\frac{\mathrm i}{|\mathbf k|^2}[(Q^{\rm ref})^{-1}]_{ki}k_j\widetilde P_{ij}^{(r)},\quad\mathbf k\ne0.\]

The wave-number factor follows directly by transforming two spatial derivatives. If the Green tensor is defined using \(k_jk_l\), its inverse already contains \(|\mathbf k|^{-2}\). If it is defined using the unit direction \(n_jn_l\), that factor must appear explicitly. The zero mode is handled separately.

Mean strain is a boundary condition.

\[\langle C_{ijkl}\rangle E_{kl}=\sigma^a_{ij}+\langle C_{ijkl}\epsilon^0_{kl}\rangle-\langle C_{ijkl}\delta\epsilon_{kl}\rangle.\]

For prescribed mean stress, invert \(\langle C\rangle\) on symmetric tensors. In general \(\langle C\rangle^{-1}\ne\langle C^{-1}\rangle\). This is the meaning of the compliance-like quantity defined in CICP Eq. (2.16). The current plate experiment instead prescribes \(E=0\); it does not claim to solve a traction-free macroscopic cell.

Elastic driving force, with the interpolation distinguished from the kernel.

\[\boxed{\frac{\delta F_e}{\delta\phi}=\frac12 e_{ij}\frac{\partial C_{ijkl}}{\partial\phi}e_{kl}-\sigma_{ij}\frac{\partial\epsilon^0_{ij}}{\partial\phi}}\]

This is the first-order result of Tushar’s Appendix B, pp. 181–182, written in indicial form. The displacement variation vanishes at mechanical equilibrium under the chosen boundary conditions. For this laboratory, \(C=C^m+h(C^p-C^m)\) and \(\epsilon^0=h\epsilon^p\), so \(g_h=\tfrac12 e:(C^p-C^m):e-\sigma:\epsilon^p\). The scalar interpolation called \(B(\phi)\) in Tushar’s thesis is not the elastic energy kernel \(B_{pq}(\mathbf n)\).

Computation

The production solver uses preconditioned conjugate gradients rather than the paper’s fixed-point iteration. Both solve the same heterogeneous equilibrium equation. An independent CICP-style spectral iteration is included in the validation suite for comparison. A spectral symmetric-gradient operator and its adjoint form A = D* C(x) D. Preconditioned conjugate gradients solve A u = D* C(x) ε⁰. A homogeneous acoustic inverse is only a preconditioner; the residual uses the full heterogeneous stiffness. Failed solves are rejected, not plotted as minima.

The unit square contains a smooth ellipse with semiaxes 0.22 and 0.045. Its diffuse area is held at π × 0.22 × 0.045 for every orientation. The tanh transition width is 0.18 in elliptic-radius units. Fourier derivatives on even grids set Nyquist derivatives to zero, consistently in both the solver and reference. These modes retain their unrelaxed energy. Refine the grid to check their influence and resolve the interface.

Circles on the plots mark sampled local minima; larger circles mark sampled global minima. Recalculate at 64² or 128² and a smaller angular step to assess convergence. The solver reports its residual and iterations. The “Homogeneous limit” copies matrix constants to the selected plate and compares against an independent Fourier-energy evaluation.

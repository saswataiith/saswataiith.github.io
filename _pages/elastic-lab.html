MODULE 0

Elastic constants and mechanical stability

Elastic constants relate elastic strain to stress: \(\sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}e_{kl}\). Here \(\sigma_{ij}\) is stress in pascals, \(e_{kl}\) is dimensionless elastic strain, and \(C_{ijkl}\) is stiffness in pascals. The indices identify Cartesian components. The elastic energy per volume is \(w=\tfrac12 C_{ijkl}e_{ij}e_{kl}\), in joules per cubic metre. The numerical examples below use a common scaled energy-density unit rather than a specified material.

Positive definite means positive elastic energy for every nonzero elastic strain. A symmetric stiffness matrix must have strictly positive eigenvalues. A zero eigenvalue is a marginal mode; a negative eigenvalue permits a strain that lowers the reference-state energy. These are small-strain elastic stability conditions for an unstressed crystal, not a complete test of lattice dynamical stability or stability under finite applied stress.

Cubic crystals: three independent stiffness constants

\[\begin{aligned}C_{11}-C_{12}&>0,\\ C_{11}+2C_{12}&>0,\\ C_{44}&>0.\end{aligned}\]

The first condition resists a volume-preserving tetragonal distortion. The second gives a positive bulk modulus \(K=(C_{11}+2C_{12})/3\), which resists uniform compression. The third resists shear in the principal planes. The normal-strain block has eigenvalues \(C_{11}+2C_{12}\) and \(C_{11}-C_{12}\), the latter occurring twice. The three engineering-shear entries are \(C_{44}\). Stiffness eigenvalues and \(K\) use the same units as the stiffnesses.

\[A_Z=\frac{2C_{44}}{C_{11}-C_{12}},\qquad A_Z=1\ \Longleftrightarrow\ C_{44}=\frac{C_{11}-C_{12}}2.\]

The dimensionless Zener ratio measures cubic elastic anisotropy. The equality gives an isotropic elastic tensor within the cubic law. A cubic crystal is not automatically isotropic.

Convert between stiffnesses and Schmidt–Gross parameters

Following I. Schmidt and D. Gross, Sandeep’s thesis uses the average in-plane shear modulus \(G\), the effective Poisson parameter \(\nu\), and \(A_Z\). Here the same average shear parameter is labelled \(\mu=G\). Specifically, \(\mu\) is the angular average of the rotated \(C_{1212}\) in the (001) plane, and \(\nu=\Lambda/[2(\Lambda+\mu)]\), where \(\Lambda\) is the angular average of the rotated \(C_{1122}\). These are averaged parameters, not the shear modulus and Poisson ratio for every direction in an anisotropic crystal.

\[\begin{aligned} C_{11}&=\mu\left[\frac{2(2+A_Z)}{1+A_Z}-\frac{1-4\nu}{1-2\nu}\right],\\ C_{12}&=\mu\left[\frac{2A_Z}{1+A_Z}-\frac{1-4\nu}{1-2\nu}\right],\\ C_{44}&=\mu\frac{2A_Z}{1+A_Z}. \end{aligned}\]

At \(A_Z=1\), the mapping becomes the usual isotropic law: \(C_{44}=\mu\), \(C_{12}=2\mu\nu/(1-2\nu)\), and \(C_{11}=2\mu(1-\nu)/(1-2\nu)\). For the anisotropic mapping with \(\mu>0\), \(A_Z>0\), and \(\nu<1/2\), cubic stability additionally requires \(1+3A_Z+4\nu>0\). Always check the converted stiffnesses. Module IV retains the requested fixed \(\nu=1/3\); the other conversion panels permit changing \(\nu\).

Physics: eigenstrain, Eshelby’s inclusion and the habit line

Eigenstrain

Eigenstrain is the stress-free transformation strain \(\epsilon^0\). When a region transforms with no external constraint, its stress stays zero:

\[\sigma=C:(\epsilon-\epsilon^0)=0,\]

where \(C\) is the elastic stiffness tensor and \(\epsilon\) the total strain. In the free region the total strain equals the transformation strain, \(\epsilon=\epsilon^0\). Examples are the lattice-misfit dilatation of a precipitate, \(\epsilon^0_{ij}=\delta_{ij}(a_p-a_m)/a_m\), thermal expansion, and the spontaneous distortion of a lattice during a phase change. In this module \(\epsilon^0=\epsilon_\eta\,\mathrm{diag}(1,t)\) in the crystal axes: principal strains \(\epsilon_1=\epsilon_\eta\) along [10] and \(\epsilon_2=t\,\epsilon_\eta\) along [01].

Eshelby’s four steps

To find the stress when the transforming region is constrained by a surrounding elastic matrix, Eshelby (1957) used a thought experiment:

  1. Cut the region Ω (the inclusion) out of the matrix and let it undergo its stress-free transformation \(\epsilon^0\).
  2. Apply surface tractions to force it back into its original hole. This imposes elastic strain \(-\epsilon^0\), i.e. stress \(-C:\epsilon^0\).
  3. Weld the interface back together.
  4. Release the tractions. The inclusion and the matrix relax together to mechanical equilibrium.

The relaxation leaves a constrained total strain \(\epsilon^c\) inside the inclusion. The elastic strain is the difference between the constrained and the stress-free geometry, and only it stores energy:

\[e=\epsilon^c-\epsilon^0,\qquad \sigma=C:e,\qquad w=\tfrac12\,e:C:e\ \ \text{(energy per unit volume)} .\]

For an ellipsoidal inclusion (an ellipse in 2-D) in a homogeneous infinite matrix, Eshelby proved that \(\epsilon^c\) is uniform and linear in the eigenstrain, \(\epsilon^c=S:\epsilon^0\). The Eshelby tensor S depends on the inclusion’s shape and orientation and on the elastic constants. This is where orientation dependence comes from: the same \(\epsilon^0\) costs different energy in differently oriented inclusions.

Thin-plate limit: a negotiation at the interface

Eshelby’s theory is a negotiation of space. The transformed region wants a new shape; the surrounding matrix resists. The theory decides who yields, and where stress is left trapped. For a very thin plate (a martensite lath, a plate-shaped precipitate) the whole negotiation happens at the flat interface. The plate is long compared with its thickness, so the matrix, which extends far beyond the plate and is unstrained away from its ends, dictates what happens along the plate. Across its thickness, the plate is free to relax.

Why jump conditions appear at an inclusion

The eigenstrain switches on abruptly at the interface: it is zero in the matrix and \(\epsilon^0\) in the plate. Suppose every strain and stress component were continuous across the interface. Hooke’s law, \(\epsilon^0=\epsilon-C^{-1}:\sigma\), would then make the eigenstrain continuous as well. It is not. So some components of strain or stress must jump. Two physical laws decide which ones:

  • No tearing, no overlapping. The interface is welded, so the displacement is continuous. This constrains the strain.
  • Newton’s third law. The force that the plate exerts on the matrix across the interface equals and opposes the force the matrix exerts on the plate. This constrains the stress.

The interface handshake

1. The matrix rules the length (Hadamard kinematics). Plate and matrix cannot separate, so their displacements match everywhere along the interface direction \(\mathbf s\). Their derivatives along \(\mathbf s\) then match too, and the plate’s total strain along the interface equals the matrix value: \(\epsilon_{ss}=0\). The plate is still free to thicken, thin or shear across its thickness (direction \(\mathbf m\)). That freedom is a jump in the displacement gradient of the form \(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m\), where \(\mathbf b\) is not yet known.

2. The forces rule the thickness (traction continuity). The forces pushing, pulling and sliding across the interface must balance. So the traction vector \(\sigma\mathbf m\), which contains the normal stress \(\sigma_{mm}\) and the shear stress \(\sigma_{sm}\), is continuous. No balance law acts on the stress that stretches the plate along its length, \(\sigma_{ss}\): it may differ completely from the matrix value.

Across the welded interface, each component is fixed by exactly one law. When the strain component is fixed, the matching stress component may jump, and the other way round:

component strain \(\epsilon\) stress \(\sigma\)
ss (along the plate) continuous: Hadamard (matches the matrix) free to jump: remnant stress
sm (shear) free to jump: set by \(\mathbf b\) continuous: traction
mm (across the plate) free to jump: set by \(\mathbf b\) continuous: traction

Counting. In 2-D a symmetric strain has 3 components. Hadamard fixes 1 of them (\(\epsilon_{ss}\)); the other 2 are carried by the 2 unknown components of \(\mathbf b\). Traction continuity supplies 2 equations (\(\sigma_{sm},\sigma_{mm}\)), exactly enough to find \(\mathbf b\). In 3-D: 6 strain components, 3 fixed in-plane by Hadamard, 3 carried by \(\mathbf b\), and 3 traction equations to find them.

Duality and the remnant stress

The unknown deformation \(\mathbf b\) and the interface traction \(\sigma\mathbf m\) are work conjugates: a movement and the force that resists it. Changing \(\mathbf b\) by \(\delta\mathbf b\) changes the plate’s energy density by \(\delta w=(\sigma\mathbf m)\cdot\delta\mathbf b\). So when the plate chooses \(\mathbf b\) to minimise its energy, the traction on its faces is driven to zero: \(\sigma_{mm}=\sigma_{sm}=0\). The plate relaxes completely across its thickness.

What is left is the stress along the plate. The plate is still held to zero length change along \(\mathbf s\), although it wanted to change by \(\epsilon^0_{ss}\). It carries the elastic strain \(e_{ss}=-\epsilon^0_{ss}\) and, because its faces are free, a single uniaxial stress

\[\sigma_{ss}=-E_s\,\epsilon^0_{ss},\qquad w=\tfrac12E_s\left(\epsilon^0_{ss}\right)^2=\tfrac12B(\mathbf m),\]

where \(E_s\) is the (plane-strain) stiffness of the crystal along \(\mathbf s\). This is the whole elastic penalty of a thin plate: the remnant stress it pays for staying compatible with the matrix. It vanishes only when \(\epsilon^0_{ss}=0\), on the habit line. In the worked case of Step 7(vii) the plate wanted to shrink by 0.005 along \(\mathbf s\), was held at its original length, and carries a tension of 0.0133.   • \(\mathbf s\): the invariant (habit) line direction, the value of \(\mathbf n\) where \(\epsilon^0_{nn}=0\). Green line on the plot.

Limits

The ideal plate theory assumes an infinitely long plate, a sharp interface, a uniform transformation strain, linear small-strain elasticity and a homogeneous stiffness. Real plates have ends, finite thickness, interfacial energy and diffuse interfaces, and plasticity can destroy coherency. In this laboratory, Module II replaces the ideal plate by a finite elliptical inclusion with Eshelby’s full solution, Module III adds elastic anisotropy, Module IV two phases with different eigenstrains, and Module V plates stiffer or softer than the matrix.

Planned extensions. Future modules will relax the remaining assumptions: inclusions stiffer or softer than the matrix through Eshelby’s equivalent-inclusion method, interfacial energy and phase-field gradients for diffuse interfaces and evolving shapes, dislocation arrays for the loss of coherency, and the 3-D invariant-plane analysis of the phenomenological theory of martensite crystallography.

Mathematics: derivation, step by step

Small strain; crystal axes x = [10], y = [01]; repeated indices summed. Vectors are columns.

Step 1. Rotate \(\epsilon^0\) into the \((\mathbf n,\mathbf q)\) frame.

In its principal frame the eigenstrain is diagonal, \(\epsilon^0=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1&0\\0&\epsilon_2\end{pmatrix}\). A frame at angle θ has basis vectors and rotation matrix (rows = new basis vectors)

\[\mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\ \sin\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf q=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\\ \cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.\]

Each new component is the tensor sandwiched between two basis vectors, e.g. \(\epsilon^0_{nq}=\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\epsilon^0\,\mathbf q=n_i\,\epsilon^0_{ij}\,q_j\). All four at once are \(\epsilon'=R\,\epsilon^0R^{\mathsf T}\). Multiply in full:

\[R\,\epsilon^0=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1\cos\theta&\epsilon_2\sin\theta\\-\epsilon_1\sin\theta&\epsilon_2\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\] \[R\,\epsilon^0R^{\mathsf T} =\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1\cos\theta&\epsilon_2\sin\theta\\-\epsilon_1\sin\theta&\epsilon_2\cos\theta\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1\cos^2\theta+\epsilon_2\sin^2\theta&(\epsilon_2-\epsilon_1)\sin\theta\cos\theta\\ (\epsilon_2-\epsilon_1)\sin\theta\cos\theta&\epsilon_1\sin^2\theta+\epsilon_2\cos^2\theta\end{pmatrix}.\]

So the entries are

\[\epsilon^0_{nn}=\epsilon_1\cos^2\theta+\epsilon_2\sin^2\theta,\qquad \epsilon^0_{qq}=\epsilon_1\sin^2\theta+\epsilon_2\cos^2\theta,\qquad \epsilon^0_{nq}=(\epsilon_2-\epsilon_1)\sin\theta\cos\theta .\]

Check: the trace \(\epsilon^0_{nn}+\epsilon^0_{qq}=\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2\) and the determinant \(\epsilon^0_{nn}\epsilon^0_{qq}-(\epsilon^0_{nq})^2=\epsilon_1\epsilon_2\) do not change with θ. For this module put \(\epsilon_1=\epsilon_\eta\), \(\epsilon_2=t\,\epsilon_\eta\): \(\epsilon^0_{nn}=\epsilon_\eta(\cos^2\theta+t\sin^2\theta)\), the curve in the “Normal eigenstrain along n(θ)” plot. At a habit root, \(\mathbf n\) becomes \(\mathbf s\) and \(\mathbf q\) becomes \(\mathbf m\).

Step 2. Eshelby’s thin plate.

(a) Hadamard compatibility. Let the interface be the plane with normal \(\mathbf m\). The displacement \(\mathbf u\) is continuous across it, so its derivative along any direction \(\mathbf s\) lying in the interface (\(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf m=0\)) is the same on both sides. The jump in the gradient therefore kills every in-plane vector:

\[[\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]\,\mathbf s=\mathbf 0\quad\text{for all }\mathbf s\perp\mathbf m \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad [\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]=\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m,\qquad \mathbf b=[\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]\,\mathbf m .\]

The matrix far from the plate ends is unstrained, so inside the plate \(\nabla\mathbf u=\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m\). Here \(\mathbf b\) is an unknown vector (a displacement per unit distance across the plate). In index notation,

\[\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}=b_i\,m_j ,\]

where i labels the displacement component and j the direction of the derivative. In 3-D, with \(\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)^{\mathsf T}\), \(\mathbf b=(b_1,b_2,b_3)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\mathbf m=(m_1,m_2,m_3)^{\mathsf T}\), the outer product is the column \(\mathbf b\) times the row \(\mathbf m^{\mathsf T}\):

\[\nabla\mathbf u= \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_1}&\dfrac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_2}&\dfrac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_3}\\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_1}&\dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_2}&\dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_3}\\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_1}&\dfrac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_2}&\dfrac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_3} \end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\\b_3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m_1&m_2&m_3\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} b_1m_1&b_1m_2&b_1m_3\\ b_2m_1&b_2m_2&b_2m_3\\ b_3m_1&b_3m_2&b_3m_3 \end{pmatrix}.\]

Every entry of the gradient is fixed by one component of \(\mathbf b\) and one of \(\mathbf m\); for example the shear gradient \(\partial u_1/\partial x_2=b_1m_2\). The matrix has rank one: every row is a multiple of \(\mathbf m^{\mathsf T}\), so \(\nabla\mathbf u\,\mathbf s=\mathbf b\,(\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf s)=\mathbf 0\) for any in-plane \(\mathbf s\). Nothing changes along the interface. In this 2-D laboratory \(m_3=b_3=0\) and the upper-left block remains:

\[\nabla\mathbf u=\begin{pmatrix}b_1m_1&b_1m_2\\b_2m_1&b_2m_2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \epsilon^c=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)=\tfrac12(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m+\mathbf m\otimes\mathbf b) =\begin{pmatrix}b_1m_1&\tfrac12(b_1m_2+b_2m_1)\\\tfrac12(b_1m_2+b_2m_1)&b_2m_2\end{pmatrix}.\]

(b) Energy. The elastic strain is \(e=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)-\epsilon^0\). With \(\sigma^0=C:\epsilon^0\) and the acoustic tensor \(Q_{ik}(\mathbf m)=C_{ijkl}m_jm_l\), and using the symmetries of C,

\[w(\mathbf b)=\tfrac12\,e:C:e =\tfrac12\Big[\epsilon^0:C:\epsilon^0-2\,\mathbf b\cdot(\sigma^0\mathbf m)+\mathbf b\cdot Q(\mathbf m)\,\mathbf b\Big].\]

(c) Traction balance. Minimising over \(\mathbf b\), \(\partial w/\partial\mathbf b=Q(\mathbf m)\mathbf b-\sigma^0\mathbf m=\mathbf 0\). This is exactly zero traction on the faces, \(\sigma\mathbf m=(C:e)\,\mathbf m=\mathbf 0\). Substituting \(\mathbf b=Q(\mathbf m)^{-1}\sigma^0\mathbf m\) back:

\[\boxed{\,w=\tfrac12\Big[\epsilon^0:C:\epsilon^0-(\sigma^0\mathbf m)\cdot Q(\mathbf m)^{-1}(\sigma^0\mathbf m)\Big]=\tfrac12\,B(\mathbf m)\,}\]

The first term is the cost of forcing the plate fully back (Eshelby’s step 2). The second is what it recovers by relaxing across its thickness (step 4). Their difference is the elastic energy kernel \(B(\mathbf m)\) of Module III and of Appendix A. In Eshelby’s language this is the thin-plate limit, with \(S:\epsilon^0=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)\).

(d) The two jump conditions, derived. Let \([\![f]\!]=f^{\rm plate}-f^{\rm matrix}\) denote a jump across the interface. Kinematic: \(\mathbf u\) is continuous, so for any in-plane \(\mathbf s\) the directional derivative is continuous, \([\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]\mathbf s=\mathbf 0\), which gives \([\![\nabla\mathbf u]\!]=\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m\) as in (a). Static: apply \(\int_{\partial P}\sigma\,\mathbf n\,dA=\mathbf 0\) to a pillbox P of face area A straddling the interface. As its height goes to zero the side faces vanish and only the two flat faces remain:

\[(\sigma^{\rm plate}-\sigma^{\rm matrix})\,\mathbf m\,A=\mathbf 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [\![\sigma]\!]\,\mathbf m=\mathbf 0 .\]

With a homogeneous C, \([\![\sigma]\!]=C:\big(\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)-[\![\epsilon^0]\!]\big)\), so the static condition reads \(Q(\mathbf m)\,\mathbf b=(C:[\![\epsilon^0]\!])\,\mathbf m\). These hold at every point of every coherent interface. For the thin plate the matrix side is unstrained and \([\![\epsilon^0]\!]=\epsilon^0\), which returns \(Q(\mathbf m)\mathbf b=\sigma^0\mathbf m\) of (c): the thin-plate solution is the pair of jump conditions.

(e) Duality. Vary \(\mathbf b\): \(\delta w=\sigma:\delta e=\sigma:\operatorname{sym}(\delta\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m) =\delta b_i\,\sigma_{ij}m_j=(\sigma\mathbf m)\cdot\delta\mathbf b\). The traction is the work conjugate of \(\mathbf b\), so \(\partial w/\partial\mathbf b=\mathbf 0\) and \(\sigma\mathbf m=\mathbf 0\) are the same statement.

(f) The remnant stress and a one-line energy. With \(\sigma\mathbf m=\mathbf 0\), the only stress left in the \((\mathbf s,\mathbf m)\) frame is \(\sigma_{ss}\): \(\sigma=\sigma_{ss}\,\mathbf s\otimes\mathbf s\). Hadamard fixes \(\epsilon_{ss}=0\), so \(e_{ss}=-\epsilon^0_{ss}\). Using the compliance \(S=C^{-1}\) (plane strain), \(e_{ss}=S_{ssss}\,\sigma_{ss}\), hence

\[\sigma_{ss}=-\frac{\epsilon^0_{ss}}{S_{ssss}}=-E_s\,\epsilon^0_{ss},\qquad w=\tfrac12\,\sigma_{ss}\,e_{ss}=\tfrac12\,E_s\left(\epsilon^0_{ss}\right)^2,\qquad \boxed{\,B(\mathbf m)=E_s\left(\epsilon^0_{ss}\right)^2\,}.\]

This is exact for any homogeneous stiffness, isotropic or anisotropic, and it makes Step 3 immediate. For the isotropic law of this page \(E_s=(C_{11}^2-C_{12}^2)/C_{11}=8/3\) in every direction, so \(B=\tfrac83(0.005)^2=6.67\times10^{-5}\) for a plate along [01] and \(\tfrac83(0.01)^2=2.67\times10^{-4}\) along [10], matching Step 7 and the Module III curve. In an anisotropic crystal \(E_s\) depends on the direction, which is how elastic anisotropy enters in Module III.

(g) Any coherent interface: the complete set of jump conditions. Steps (a)–(f) used a thin plate, but nothing in the jump conditions depends on the plate. They hold at every point of any perfectly bonded interface between an inclusion with uniform eigenstrain \(\epsilon^0_{ij}\) (often written \(\epsilon^*_{ij}\)) and a matrix with the same stiffness \(C_{ijkl}\). Let \(n_i\) be the outward unit normal of the inclusion and \([\![A]\!]=A^{\rm in}-A^{\rm out}\), the convention used on this page.

1. Displacement gradient. \(u_i\) is continuous, so only the derivative across the interface can jump (Hadamard):

\[[\![u_{i,j}]\!]=g_i\,n_j .\]

2. Strain. The total strain is the symmetric part of the gradient, and the eigenstrain jumps by its full value:

\[[\![\epsilon_{ij}]\!]=\tfrac12\left(g_i n_j+g_j n_i\right),\qquad [\![\epsilon^0_{ij}]\!]=\epsilon^0_{ij},\qquad [\![e_{ij}]\!]=\tfrac12\left(g_i n_j+g_j n_i\right)-\epsilon^0_{ij}.\]

3. Stress. With the same \(C_{ijkl}\) on both sides and its minor symmetry,

\[[\![\sigma_{ij}]\!]=C_{ijkl}[\![e_{kl}]\!]=C_{ijkl}\left(g_k n_l-\epsilon^0_{kl}\right),\qquad [\![\sigma_{ij}]\!]\,n_j=0\ \ \text{(Newton’s third law)}.\]

4. Solve for the amplitude. Substitute the stress jump into traction continuity and use the acoustic (Christoffel) tensor \(K_{ik}=C_{ijkl}n_jn_l\):

\[C_{ijkl}\,n_j n_l\,g_k=C_{ijkl}\,\epsilon^0_{kl}\,n_j \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_i=K^{-1}_{ik}\,C_{kjml}\,\epsilon^0_{ml}\,n_j=\big[Q(\mathbf n)^{-1}\sigma^0\mathbf n\big]_i .\]

So the jumps are fixed locally by the normal, the stiffness and the eigenstrain alone, whatever the shape of the inclusion. The shape only decides the field on one side (Module II computes it for an ellipse). Texts that define the jump as outside minus inside write \(\lambda_i=-g_i\), and every jump below changes sign.

For the thin plate, the matrix side is unstrained, so the plate’s own field equals the jump and \(\mathbf b=\mathbf g\).

Step 3. \(B(\mathbf m)=0\iff\epsilon^0_{ss}=0\).

(⇒) If \(B(\mathbf m)=0\) then \(w=0\), and because C is positive definite, \(e=0\): the total strain equals the transformation strain, \(\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m)=\epsilon^0\). Project along the interface direction \(\mathbf s\) (\(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf m=0\)):

\[\epsilon^0_{ss}=\mathbf s\cdot\Big[\tfrac12(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf m+\mathbf m\otimes\mathbf b)\Big]\mathbf s =\tfrac12\big[(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf b)(\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf s)+(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf m)(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf s)\big]=0 .\]

(⇐) If \(\epsilon^0_{ss}=0\), write both sides in the \((\mathbf s,\mathbf m)\) frame, where \(\mathbf m=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}\):

\[\epsilon'=\begin{pmatrix}0&\epsilon^0_{sm}\\\epsilon^0_{sm}&\epsilon^0_{mm}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \operatorname{sym}(\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf m)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\tfrac12a_s\\\tfrac12a_s&a_m\end{pmatrix}.\]

They match with \(a_s=2\epsilon^0_{sm}\), \(a_m=\epsilon^0_{mm}\). Then \(\mathbf b=\mathbf a\) gives \(e=0\) and \(B(\mathbf m)=0\). The condition is kinematic: it holds for every stiffness C. The elastic constants only decide how costly the other orientations are.

With \(\epsilon_1=\epsilon_\eta\), \(\epsilon_2=t\,\epsilon_\eta\) and \(\cos\theta\ne0\):

\[\epsilon^0_{ss}=\epsilon_\eta\left(\cos^2\theta+t\sin^2\theta\right)=0 \iff\tan^2\theta=-\frac1t \iff\theta_1=\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{-t}},\quad\theta_2=180^\circ-\theta_1 .\]

The habit condition can be written for the line or for the normal. Both routes give the same plate.

Route A: start from the line. Step 4 gave the line angle through \(\tan\theta_1=1/\sqrt{-t}\). The normal is the line turned by 90°:

\[\mathbf s=(\cos\theta_1,\ \sin\theta_1),\qquad \mathbf m=(-\sin\theta_1,\ \cos\theta_1).\]

\[\tan\alpha=\frac{1}{\tan\theta_1}=\frac{1}{1/\sqrt{-t}}=\sqrt{-t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha=\tan^{-1}\sqrt{-t}=90^\circ-\theta_1 .\]

Why did this happen?

Physics: the inclusion, its interface and its limits

Eshelby’s uniform interior

Module I described Eshelby’s cut, force back, weld and release experiment. Its remarkable result is that for an ellipse (an ellipsoid in 3-D) in a homogeneous infinite matrix, the constrained strain inside is uniform, whatever the aspect ratio and orientation. The inclusion also turns rigidly by a small angle ω. The panel on the left shows three outlines: the hole, the shape the inclusion would take if it were free, and the shape it actually takes.

The interface is local

At every point of the interface the two jump conditions of Module I hold, with the local normal \(\mathbf m\) and tangent \(\mathbf s\): welding keeps the displacement continuous, so \(\epsilon_{ss}\) is continuous; force balance keeps the traction \(\sigma\mathbf m\) continuous, so \(\sigma_{sm}\) and \(\sigma_{mm}\) are continuous. The other components, \(\epsilon_{sm}\), \(\epsilon_{mm}\) and \(\sigma_{ss}\), jump. The size of the jump depends only on \(\mathbf m\), the eigenstrain and the stiffness. It does not depend on the inclusion’s shape. The shape only sets the uniform field inside; the field just outside is that interior field minus the jump.

From circle to thin plate

For a circle the matrix pushes back from every direction, and the interior keeps a mix of all components. As the ellipse flattens, its long faces dominate. The matrix beside those faces becomes unstrained, so the interior field becomes exactly the jump across the faces: the thin plate of Module I. Its energy per unit area approaches \(\tfrac12B(\mathbf m)\). If the plate lies along a habit line, that limit is zero; otherwise a remnant stress \(\sigma_{ss}\) stays trapped along the plate.

Orientation

Rotating the ellipse changes which direction carries the long interface. For a thin plate the energy follows \(\tfrac12B(\mathbf m)\) and is lowest at the habit orientation. A rounder inclusion averages over many normals, so its energy depends less on orientation.

Mathematics: interior field, jumps and limits

Step 1. The interior displacement gradient.

Let the ellipse have semi-axes a (along the angle φ) and b. For a direction n write \(Q(\mathbf n)_{ik}=C_{ijkl}n_jn_l\), \(\sigma^0=C:\epsilon^0\) and

\[\mathbf g(\mathbf n)=Q(\mathbf n)^{-1}\,\sigma^0\mathbf n .\]

The Fourier solution of equilibrium gives the total strain of any inclusion as \(\hat\epsilon(\mathbf k)=\operatorname{sym}\big(\mathbf g(\hat{\mathbf k})\otimes\hat{\mathbf k}\big)\,\hat\theta(\mathbf k)\), where \(\theta\) is 1 inside and 0 outside. For an ellipse the transform of θ depends on \(\mathbf k\) only through one elliptic radius, and the inverse transform at any interior point reduces to an average over directions (Mura, §11):

\[D=\nabla\mathbf u^{\rm in}=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\mathbf g(\mathbf n)\otimes\mathbf n\,d\vartheta,\qquad \mathbf n\parallel R(\varphi)\begin{pmatrix}\cos\vartheta/a\\ \sin\vartheta/b\end{pmatrix}.\]

From D follow the interior strain, rotation, stress and energy:

\[\epsilon^c=\operatorname{sym}D=S:\epsilon^0,\qquad \omega=\tfrac12(D_{yx}-D_{xy}),\qquad \sigma^{\rm in}=C:(\epsilon^c-\epsilon^0),\qquad w=-\tfrac12\,\sigma^{\rm in}:\epsilon^0 .\]

w is Eshelby’s elastic energy per unit inclusion area (matrix energy included). The integral reproduces Mura’s closed-form isotropic Eshelby tensor to machine precision, and it works unchanged for the anisotropic cubic law.

Step 2. The jump at a boundary point.

At a point with outward normal \(\mathbf m\), Hadamard’s condition gives \([\![\epsilon]\!]=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf g\otimes\mathbf m)\) for some \(\mathbf g\), and traction continuity \([\![\sigma]\!]\mathbf m=\mathbf 0\) with \([\![\sigma]\!]=C:([\![\epsilon]\!]-\epsilon^0)\) gives \(Q(\mathbf m)\mathbf g=\sigma^0\mathbf m\). So

\[\epsilon^{\rm out}=\epsilon^c-\operatorname{sym}\big(\mathbf g(\mathbf m)\otimes\mathbf m\big),\qquad \sigma^{\rm out}=C:\epsilon^{\rm out},\qquad [\![\sigma]\!]=C:\big(\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf g\otimes\mathbf m)-\epsilon^0\big).\]

The jump is the thin-plate field of Module I for the normal \(\mathbf m\): it is fixed locally, independent of shape. The table rotates each tensor into the \((\mathbf s,\mathbf m)\) frame with the sandwich rule of Module I, Step 1.

Step 3. The two limits.

Thin plate (\(b/a\to0\)): \(\mathbf n\) points along \(\pm\mathbf m_0\), the normal of the long faces, for almost every \(\vartheta\). Then \(D\to\mathbf g(\mathbf m_0)\otimes\mathbf m_0\), the field outside the faces vanishes, and \(w\to\tfrac12B(\mathbf m_0)\). Circle (\(b=a\)): \(\mathbf n\) is spread evenly; for isotropic stiffness and a dilatation \(\epsilon^0=\epsilon\,I\) (plane strain) the interior strain is \(\epsilon^c=\epsilon\,I/[2(1-\nu)]\).

Step 4. The field maps.

The maps solve the same equation on a periodic 256 × 256 cell: \(\hat\epsilon(\mathbf k)=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf g(\hat{\mathbf k})\otimes\hat{\mathbf k})\hat\theta(\mathbf k)\) for \(\mathbf k\ne0\), mean total strain zero, and \(\sigma=C:(\epsilon-\theta\epsilon^0)\). The profiles plot this numerical field along \(\mathbf m\) through the chosen point, with the exact inside and outside values of Step 2 dotted.

Why did this happen?

Physics

Eigenstrain specifies what the phase wants to do. Stiffness specifies the cost of preventing it. Mechanical relaxation reduces that cost. Their combination gives B(n). A minimum identifies a low-penalty modulation normal; the corresponding ideal plate is perpendicular to it. Several equal minima are symmetry-related choices.

Mathematics

From my Appendix A, Eqs. (A.1–A.12), pp. 107–111. This is the elastic-energy derivation developed using Khachaturyan’s microelasticity framework. Spatial indices \(i,j,k,l\) are summed; phase indices \(p,q\) are summed only where shown. The laboratory uses two spatial dimensions.

1. Separate eigenstrain, mean strain and periodic relaxation.

\[\epsilon^0_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\sum_p\theta_p(\mathbf r)\epsilon^{(p)}_{ij},\qquad e_{ij}=E_{ij}+\tfrac12(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i})-\epsilon^0_{ij}.\]\[F_e=\tfrac12\int_V C_{ijkl}e_{ij}e_{kl}\,dV.\]

For homogeneous stiffness and zero applied mean stress, \(E_{ij}=\langle\epsilon^0_{ij}\rangle\). The mean terms cancel in the six-integral expansion of Appendix A. Only the fluctuations \(\Delta\theta_p=\theta_p-\langle\theta_p\rangle\) remain.

2. Solve equilibrium for each nonzero Fourier mode.

\[\widehat\sigma^{(p)}_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\epsilon^{(p)}_{kl},\quad Q_{ik}(\mathbf n)=C_{ijkl}n_jn_l,\quad\Omega_{ik}=(Q^{-1})_{ik}.\]\[\widetilde u_k=-\frac{\mathrm i}{|\mathbf k|^2}\Omega_{ki}(\mathbf n)k_j\sum_p\widehat\sigma^{(p)}_{ij}\widetilde\theta_p,\qquad\mathbf n=\mathbf k/|\mathbf k|.\]

The acoustic contraction leaves \(i,k\) free. It is not the contraction \(C_{ijkl}n_kn_l\), which leaves a different index pair free.

3. Substitute the relaxed displacement into the energy.

\[\boxed{B_{pq}(\mathbf n)=C_{ijkl}\epsilon^{(p)}_{ij}\epsilon^{(q)}_{kl}-n_j\widehat\sigma^{(p)}_{ij}\Omega_{ki}(\mathbf n)\widehat\sigma^{(q)}_{kl}n_l}\]\[F_e=\frac12\sum_{p,q}\int_{\mathbf k\ne0} B_{pq}(\mathbf n)\widetilde\theta_p(\mathbf k)\widetilde\theta_q^*(\mathbf k)\frac{d^d k}{(2\pi)^d}.\]

\(B_{pq}\) is the elastic energy kernel. Its first term is the unrelaxed eigenstrain contraction; the second is the energy reduction from mechanical relaxation. It becomes a total energy only after weighting by the field spectra and summing or integrating over modes. This is Eq. (A.12), with \(d=2\) here.

4. Differentiate the energy: Appendix B, Eqs. (B.1–B.12).

\[g_a=\frac{\delta F_e}{\delta\theta_a},\qquad \widetilde g_a=\sum_q B_{aq}\widetilde\theta_q.\]

Here \(a\) is a fixed field label, not a summed spatial index. The two equal variations of the quadratic form cancel its factor of one half. We use \(B_{aq}=B_{qa}\), renaming dummy indices explicitly. For \(\theta_a=\eta_a^2\), the chain rule gives \(\delta F_e/\delta\eta_a=2\eta_a g_a\) in real space.

Boundary-condition distinction: Appendix A uses a relaxed mean strain. The finite-plate module fixes \(E_{ij}=0\) and therefore retains the additional mean energy \(\tfrac{V}{2}(E-\langle\epsilon^0\rangle):C:(E-\langle\epsilon^0\rangle)\). Its zero mode must not be silently dropped.

\(A_Z=2C_{44}/(C_{11}-C_{12})\). The 2-D law uses the in-plane cubic sub-block, with tensor shear \(\sigma_{xy}=2C_{44}e_{xy}\), no out-of-plane eigenstrain, and no plane-stress condensation.

Why did this happen?

When is the β–γ cross coefficient important?

The cross coefficient measures elastic coupling between the two phase fields. It is not the interaction energy of two particles. For the homogeneous elastic constants and dilatational misfits used here, all three kernels contain the same directional function:

\[B_{\beta\beta}(\mathbf n)=\epsilon_\beta^2K(\mathbf n),\qquad B_{\gamma\gamma}(\mathbf n)=\epsilon_\gamma^2K(\mathbf n),\qquad B_{\beta\gamma}(\mathbf n)=\epsilon_\beta\epsilon_\gamma K(\mathbf n).\]

Here \(\epsilon_\beta\) and \(\epsilon_\gamma\) are dimensionless linear misfits relative to the matrix. The unit vector \(\mathbf n\) gives the Fourier wavevector direction. The function \(K\) is the elastic kernel for unit dilatational misfit; it depends on the elastic constants and direction. The kernels have the dimensions of energy per volume when dimensional stiffnesses are used. This widget displays them in the model energy-density units defined above.

\[|B_{\beta\gamma}|=\sqrt{B_{\beta\beta}B_{\gamma\gamma}}.\]

Thus the cross coefficient is comparable to both self coefficients when the misfit magnitudes are comparable. For Sandeep’s equal-and-opposite example, \(\epsilon_\beta=0.01\) and \(\epsilon_\gamma=-0.01\), so \(B_{\beta\gamma}=-B_{\beta\beta}=-B_{\gamma\gamma}\) at every direction. There is no anisotropy threshold at which the cross coefficient suddenly appears.

A large cross coefficient does not by itself establish a large particle interaction. The interaction energy also depends on the amounts, shapes, separation and arrangement of both phase fields. Contributions from different Fourier modes can reinforce or cancel. Elastic anisotropy gives the kernel angular variation; a nonzero isotropic cross coefficient alone does not establish an arrangement-dependent interaction. The Bitter–Crum derivation below explains the isotropic limit.

However, \(B_{\beta\gamma}(\mathbf n)\) is a reciprocal-space coefficient. To obtain real-space interaction energy at separation R, we must also include particle shape spectra and Fourier phases. Single-particle energy and pair-interaction energy are different quantities.

When does the interaction control the arrangement?

For the two prescribed circular profiles in this widget, rotating their separation leaves each self energy unchanged. The angle-dependent interaction therefore selects the preferred separation direction. In the thesis example, opposite misfits with \(A_Z=3\) give the lowest pair interaction along [11]. The self kernels still favour Fourier normals along [10].

Use the pair calculation to compare actual arrangements, rather than reading a pair direction directly from the kernel polar plot. Define

\[\Delta E_{\mathrm{int}}=E_{\mathrm{int}}([11])-E_{\mathrm{int}}([10]).\]

Here \(E_{\mathrm{int}}\) is the interaction energy reported by this widget in its normalized model units. The directions [10] and [11] describe the line joining the particle centres; they are different from the Fourier normal \(\mathbf n\) in the kernel plots. A negative difference favours [11], a positive difference favours [10], and zero gives no preference between these two arrangements. This comparison keeps particle size, separation and elastic constants fixed. It does not exclude a lower energy at another angle.

In an evolving microstructure, the precipitates can also change shape and composition. The change in interaction energy must then be compared with changes in self elastic energy, interfacial energy and chemical free energy. Larger misfits increase elastic effects relative to the nonelastic contributions when other parameters are fixed. Both precipitate phases must have appreciable misfits and spatially coupled fields for their cross contribution to be important. Increasing anisotropy can strengthen a directional preference, but it does not give a universal threshold for interaction dominance.

For one Fourier mode, define \(S_\beta=B_{\beta\beta}|\hat h_\beta|^2\), \(S_\gamma=B_{\gamma\gamma}|\hat h_\gamma|^2\), and \(X=2B_{\beta\gamma}\operatorname{Re}(\hat h_\beta\hat h_\gamma^*)\). The mode energy is \((S_\beta+S_\gamma+X)/2\). The cross contribution is most significant when both self contributions are comparable and the Fourier phases give strong correlation. Mechanical stability gives \(|X|\leq S_\beta+S_\gamma\): the interaction can control the preferred arrangement without exceeding the sum of the self contributions in magnitude. The full phase-field calculation is needed to decide the final morphology.

Derivation and the Bitter–Crum limit

1. Write the transformation strain of each phase

Let \(h_\beta(\mathbf x)\) and \(h_\gamma(\mathbf x)\) be dimensionless fields describing where the precipitates are located. A field is one inside its phase and zero outside for a sharp interface; a smooth field can represent a diffuse interface. The position is \(\mathbf x\). Taking the matrix as the zero-misfit reference gives

\[\epsilon^0_{ij}(\mathbf x)=\epsilon^{\beta}_{ij}h_\beta(\mathbf x)+\epsilon^{\gamma}_{ij}h_\gamma(\mathbf x),\qquad \epsilon^p_{ij}=\epsilon_p\delta_{ij},\quad p\in\{\beta,\gamma\}.\]

Here \(\epsilon^0_{ij}\) is the prescribed transformation strain, and \(\delta_{ij}\) is one when \(i=j\) and zero otherwise. The indices identify Cartesian components. A three-dimensional dilatation has three equal diagonal components. This widget uses the two-dimensional in-plane elastic law described in Module III: \(\boldsymbol\epsilon^p=\epsilon_p\operatorname{diag}(1,1)\). It omits the out-of-plane transformation strain; it is not a full three-dimensional plane-strain reduction.

2. Impose mechanical equilibrium

The displacement components are \(u_i\). The total strain is \(\epsilon_{ij}=(\partial_i u_j+\partial_j u_i)/2\), where \(\partial_i\) denotes differentiation with respect to position component \(x_i\). Elastic strain is total strain minus transformation strain. Stress \(\sigma_{ij}\) and elastic energy \(E_{\mathrm{el}}\) are therefore

\[\sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}(\epsilon_{kl}-\epsilon^0_{kl}),\qquad \partial_j\sigma_{ij}=0,\]\[E_{\mathrm{el}}=\frac12\int_V(\epsilon_{ij}-\epsilon^0_{ij})C_{ijkl}(\epsilon_{kl}-\epsilon^0_{kl})\,dV.\]

Repeated indices are summed. \(V\) is the body volume; the two-dimensional calculation uses area and energy per unit out-of-plane thickness. Stiffness and stress have units of pressure in a dimensional model, and the integral then has units of energy. Mechanical equilibrium assumes no body force. The displacement adjusts to reduce the energy while satisfying the boundary conditions.

3. Solve each nonzero Fourier mode

A Fourier mode has wavevector \(\mathbf k\), with units of inverse length in a dimensional model. Its unit normal is \(\mathbf n=\mathbf k/|\mathbf k|\). Define the stress associated with the prescribed strain, the acoustic tensor, and the inverse acoustic tensor by

\[\sigma^p_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\epsilon^p_{kl},\qquad Q_{ik}(\mathbf n)=C_{ijkl}n_jn_l,\qquad \Omega_{ik}(\mathbf n)=[Q(\mathbf n)^{-1}]_{ik}.\]

\(Q\) describes the directional stiffness of the mode. \(\Omega\) has units of inverse stiffness. With the Fourier convention \(\widehat{\partial_j u_i}=\mathrm i k_j\hat u_i\), mechanical equilibrium gives

\[|\mathbf k|^2Q_{ik}\hat u_k=-\mathrm i|\mathbf k|n_j\hat\sigma^0_{ij},\qquad \hat u_i=-\frac{\mathrm i}{|\mathbf k|}\Omega_{im}n_j\hat\sigma^0_{mj}.\]

Here \(\mathrm i^2=-1\), a hat denotes a Fourier coefficient, and \(\hat\sigma^0_{ij}=\sum_p\sigma^p_{ij}\hat h_p\). Substituting this equilibrium displacement into the energy removes the displacement as an independent unknown.

4. Obtain the two self kernels and one cross kernel

\[B_{pq}(\mathbf n)=\epsilon^p_{ij}C_{ijkl}\epsilon^q_{kl}-a^p_i\Omega_{im}a^q_m,\qquad a^p_i=\sigma^p_{ij}n_j.\]

The first term is the energy coefficient before displacement relaxation. The second term is the reduction produced by mechanical relaxation. The vector \(a^p_i\) is the traction associated with the prescribed strain on a plane normal to \(\mathbf n\). Each \(B_{pq}\) has units of energy density in a dimensional model.

For clarity, define \(\hat h_p(\mathbf k)=V^{-1}\int_V h_p(\mathbf x)e^{-\mathrm i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\,dV\) in a periodic body. These coefficients are dimensionless. The nonzero-mode energy density is

\[\frac{E_{\mathrm{el}}^{\ne0}}{V}=\frac12\sum_{\mathbf k\ne0}\left[B_{\beta\beta}|\hat h_\beta|^2+B_{\gamma\gamma}|\hat h_\gamma|^2+2B_{\beta\gamma}\operatorname{Re}(\hat h_\beta\hat h_\gamma^*)\right].\]

\(\operatorname{Re}\) means real part, and an asterisk means complex conjugate. The two self kernels describe each precipitate phase’s contribution. The cross kernel describes the coupling between their fields. Symmetry gives \(B_{\beta\gamma}=B_{\gamma\beta}\), so there are three independent kernels, although the system contains three phases. The matrix contributes through the common stiffness and the reference strain.

For the two-dimensional square-symmetric stiffness, write \(n_x=\cos\theta\) and \(n_y=\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the wavevector-normal angle. The matrix actually inverted by the widget is

\[Q=\begin{pmatrix}C_{11}n_x^2+C_{44}n_y^2&(C_{12}+C_{44})n_xn_y\\(C_{12}+C_{44})n_xn_y&C_{44}n_x^2+C_{11}n_y^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad A_Z=\frac{2C_{44}}{C_{11}-C_{12}}.\]

The thesis parameters give \(C_{11}=7000\), \(C_{12}=5000\), \(C_{44}=3000\) in model units. The equal and opposite misfits imply \(B_{\beta\beta}=B_{\gamma\gamma}\) and \(B_{\beta\gamma}=-B_{\beta\beta}\). For this in-plane calculation, the self coefficient is \(0.342857\) along [10] and \(0.8\) along [11]; the cross coefficient is their negative. These numbers check the kernel calculation, not the reproduction of a complete thesis microstructure.

5. Derive the Bitter–Crum theorem from the isotropic kernel

The isotropic example demonstrates the Bitter–Crum theorem. We can show why it holds by calculating \(B_{pq}\). Assume that the elastic moduli are the same in all phases, the elasticity is isotropic, and each phase has a purely dilatational transformation strain. Keep the phase fractions and misfits fixed. Use an infinite body or the compatible periodic boundary conditions described below.

For the two-dimensional in-plane law used here, let \(\lambda=C_{12}\) and \(\mu=C_{44}\) be the Lamé elastic constants. Both have units of pressure in a dimensional model. Isotropy requires \(C_{11}=\lambda+2\mu\), or \(A_Z=1\). Since \(\epsilon^p_{ij}=\epsilon_p\delta_{ij}\), the prescribed stress is \(\sigma^p_{ij}=2(\lambda+\mu)\epsilon_p\delta_{ij}\). Thus

\[Q=\mu I+(\lambda+\mu)\mathbf n\otimes\mathbf n,\qquad Q^{-1}\mathbf n=\frac{\mathbf n}{\lambda+2\mu},\qquad \mathbf a^p=2(\lambda+\mu)\epsilon_p\mathbf n.\]

Here \(I\) is the identity matrix, and \(\mathbf n\otimes\mathbf n\) has components \(n_i n_j\). Substitute these expressions into the kernel. The two terms give

\[B_{pq}=\left[4(\lambda+\mu)-\frac{4(\lambda+\mu)^2}{\lambda+2\mu}\right]\epsilon_p\epsilon_q =\underbrace{\frac{4\mu(\lambda+\mu)}{\lambda+2\mu}}_{K_B}\epsilon_p\epsilon_q =\frac{C_{11}^2-C_{12}^2}{C_{11}}\epsilon_p\epsilon_q.\]

The coefficient \(K_B\) has units of pressure. There is no direction \(\mathbf n\) left in this expression. Equal and opposite misfits give \(B_{\beta\gamma}=-B_{\beta\beta}\), but this negative coefficient alone does not imply attraction between precipitates. We must substitute it into the complete energy expression.

Define the scalar misfit field \(e(\mathbf x)=\epsilon_\beta h_\beta(\mathbf x)+\epsilon_\gamma h_\gamma(\mathbf x)\). It is dimensionless. Parseval’s identity states that the sum of the squared Fourier coefficients equals the spatial average of the squared field. Removing the zero mode therefore gives

\[\frac{E_{\mathrm{el}}^{\ne0}}{V}=\frac{K_B}{2}\sum_{\mathbf k\ne0}|\hat e(\mathbf k)|^2 =\frac{K_B}{2}\left(\langle e^2\rangle-\langle e\rangle^2\right).\]

Angle brackets denote the spatial average. For disjoint phase regions, \(h_p\) is one inside phase \(p\) and zero outside. Consequently \(h_p^2=h_p\) and \(h_\beta h_\gamma=0\). If \(f_p=\langle h_p\rangle\) is the dimensionless phase fraction, then

\[\frac{E_{\mathrm{el}}^{\ne0}}{V}=\frac{K_B}{2}\left[f_\beta\epsilon_\beta^2+f_\gamma\epsilon_\gamma^2- (f_\beta\epsilon_\beta+f_\gamma\epsilon_\gamma)^2\right].\]

This is the Bitter–Crum result in this model. The energy depends on the phase fractions and misfits. It does not depend on precipitate shape, position, separation or arrangement. Moving disjoint precipitates while keeping their amounts fixed does not change the energy: there is no elastic interaction driving their relative motion. Opposite misfits do not change this conclusion.

For imposed mean strain \(\bar\epsilon\), add \(E_0/V=\tfrac12(\bar\epsilon-\langle\epsilon^0\rangle):C:(\bar\epsilon-\langle\epsilon^0\rangle)\). The colons denote tensor contraction. This term also depends only on the mean transformation strain when the imposed strain is fixed. At relaxed zero mean stress, it vanishes. Comparing arrangements at fixed phase amounts is essential; a cross term obtained by subtracting two separate clamped calculations is not, by itself, a separation-dependent interaction.

Select Isotropic comparison to check the constant kernel numerically. Its circle and the horizontal signed curves demonstrate the directional cancellation derived above; the energy reduction proves the shape-and-arrangement independence. With anisotropic moduli, the kernel generally retains its directional dependence, and this Bitter–Crum proof no longer applies. Eshelby’s uniform-interior-strain theorem for an ellipsoidal inclusion is a separate result.

Why did this happen?

Physics

Soft and hard refer to the elastic response relative to the matrix. The same eigenstrain is accommodated differently when stiffness varies in space.

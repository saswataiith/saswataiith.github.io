Conventions before calculation.

The derivation assumes linear elasticity, homogeneous stiffness \(C_{ijkl}\), periodic fields and zero applied mean stress. We write

\[\varepsilon^0_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\sum_p\theta_p(\mathbf r)\,\varepsilon^{(p)}_{ij},\qquad \theta_0=c,\qquad \theta_i=\eta_i^2\quad(i=1,2,3).\]

The mean strain relaxes to \(\langle\varepsilon^0_{ij}\rangle\). The elastic energy is therefore determined by the fluctuating fields

\[\Delta\theta_p(\mathbf r)=\theta_p(\mathbf r)-\langle\theta_p\rangle.\]

Our Fourier-transform convention is

\[\widetilde{\theta}_p(\mathbf k)=\int_{\mathbb R^d}\theta_p(\mathbf r)e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^d r,\qquad \theta_p(\mathbf r)=\int_{\mathbb R^d}\widetilde{\theta}_p(\mathbf k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\,\frac{d^d k}{(2\pi)^d}.\]

The thesis writes the general Fourier integrals with \(d=3\), while the simulated model is two-dimensional. We retain \(d\) to keep these settings distinct. A prime on a Fourier integral, \(\int^{\prime}\), excludes the zero mode \(\mathbf k=\mathbf 0\).

Source: Saswata Bhattacharyya, Evolution of Multivariant Microstructures with Anisotropic Misfit: A Phase Field Study, Appendix A, printed pp. 107–111; Appendix B, pp. 112–114; §3.4, pp. 31–32. Notation is streamlined here; this is a teaching adaptation, not a verbatim transcription.

APPENDIX A · EQUATIONS A.1–A.12

From mechanical equilibrium to \(B_{pq}\).

1. Separate the mean and periodic strain.

\[\begin{aligned}\boldsymbol\epsilon^{\mathrm{el}}&=\boldsymbol\epsilon^{\mathrm{per}}+\mathbf E-\boldsymbol\epsilon^0\\ \mathbf E&=\langle\boldsymbol\epsilon^0\rangle,\quad\langle\boldsymbol\epsilon^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle=0\\F_e&=\frac12\int_V(\boldsymbol\epsilon^{\mathrm{per}}-\Delta\boldsymbol\epsilon^0):\mathbf C:(\boldsymbol\epsilon^{\mathrm{per}}-\Delta\boldsymbol\epsilon^0)\,dV\end{aligned}\]

The mean terms cancel because the mean strain can relax. This cancellation would change under imposed mean strain or other loading conditions.

Follow the six-integral expansion in the thesis

Appendix A expands the original expression as Fₑ = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ + I₄ + I₅ + I₆. The periodic-strain/mean-strain term I₂ vanishes. I₄ + I₅ cancel the mean-eigenstrain part of I₆. What remains combines periodic elastic relaxation with the eigenstrain fluctuations. This is why the final integral omits the zero mode.

I₁ contains one half of the relaxation quadratic form, while I₃ contains its negative with no factor of one half. Their sum therefore supplies the negative one-half relaxation contribution in the final kernel.

2. Solve the equilibrium equation mode by mode.

For k ≠ 0, define n = k/|k|, σ⁽ᵖ⁾ = C:ε⁽ᵖ⁾ and the acoustic tensor Q. Let a⁽ᵖ⁾ = σ⁽ᵖ⁾n. Mechanical equilibrium eliminates displacement through the inverse acoustic tensor Ω = Q⁻¹.

\[\begin{aligned}Q_{ik}(\mathbf n)&=C_{ijkl}n_jn_l\\\boldsymbol\Omega&=\mathbf Q^{-1},\qquad\mathbf a^{(p)}=\boldsymbol\sigma^{(p)}\mathbf n\\\widetilde{\mathbf u}(\mathbf k)&=-\frac{i}{|\mathbf k|}\boldsymbol\Omega(\mathbf n)\sum_p\mathbf a^{(p)}(\mathbf n)\Delta\widetilde\theta_p(\mathbf k)\end{aligned}\]

With the Fourier convention above, differentiating displacement multiplies its transform by +ik. The zero mode is handled by the mean-strain condition, not by inverting Q at k = 0.

3. Substitute the relaxed strain into the energy.

\[\begin{aligned}B_{pq}(\mathbf n)&=\boldsymbol\epsilon^{(p)}:\mathbf C:\boldsymbol\epsilon^{(q)}-\mathbf a^{(p)}\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\mathbf a^{(q)}\\F_e&=\frac12\sum_{p,q}\int^{\prime}B_{pq}(\mathbf n)\widetilde\theta_p(\mathbf k)\widetilde\theta_q^{\,*}(\mathbf k)\frac{d^d k}{(2\pi)^d}\end{aligned}\]

The first term is the unrelaxed eigenstrain contribution. The second subtracts the energy released by a compatible displacement field. For a stable stiffness tensor, the relaxed quadratic form is nonnegative. Bₚq = Bqₚ and Bₚq(n) = Bₚq(−n).

The thesis’s Appendix A arrives at this expression in Eq. (A.12), using Parseval’s theorem and the cancellation of the mean terms.

APPENDIX B · EQUATIONS B.1–B.12

Vary both factors.

Varying the quadratic energy produces two terms: one from θ̃ₚ and one from θ̃*q. Real fields obey θ̃*(k) = θ̃(−k). Combining this reality condition with the evenness and symmetry of B makes the two half-contributions equal.

\[\begin{aligned}g_i(\mathbf r)&\equiv\frac{\delta F_e}{\delta\theta_i(\mathbf r)}\\\widetilde g_i(\mathbf k)&=\sum_p B_{ip}(\mathbf n)\widetilde\theta_p(\mathbf k)\\g_i(\mathbf r)&=\mathcal F^{-1}\!\left[\sum_p B_{ip}\widetilde\theta_p\right]\end{aligned}\]

The factor ½ in the energy therefore disappears from its derivative. Here i labels the selected field; p is summed. We write the symmetry explicitly as Bₚᵢ = Bᵢₚ to avoid confusing the free field index with a summation index.

Return to composition and structural order.

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\delta(F_e/N_v)}{\delta c}&=\frac{g_0}{N_v}\\\frac{\delta(F_e/N_v)}{\delta\eta_i}&=\frac{2\eta_i(\mathbf r)g_i(\mathbf r)}{N_v}\end{aligned}\]

These are the chain-rule results in Eqs. (3.36–3.37). Nᵥ is the atomic number density used in the thesis normalization. For ηᵢ, first compute gᵢ by inverse FFT, then multiply by 2ηᵢ in real space. Multiplication of those two fields is a convolution in Fourier space; multiplying their Fourier transforms pointwise is incorrect.

Keep the derivative and the evolution sign distinct.

The elastic chemical-potential contribution enters the conserved equation as M∇²(g₀/Nᵥ) for constant mobility. The elastic contribution to the nonconserved order-parameter rate is −Lᵢ(2ηᵢgᵢ/Nᵥ). These are elastic contributions only; chemical and gradient-energy terms must also be included.

The same calculation in both languages.

Assume four real θ fields and a precomputed real, even, symmetric kernel with B(k = 0) = 0. These snippets compute gᵢ = δFₑ/δθᵢ; they do not construct the kernel or advance time. NumPy uses axes (field, x, y); Julia uses (x, y, field).

NumPy: sum over coupled fields, then inverse transform
# theta.shape = (4, nx, ny); B.shape = (4, 4, nx, ny)
# eta.shape = (3, nx, ny); theta[0]=c; theta[1:]=eta**2
h = np.fft.fft2(theta, axes=(-2, -1))
g_hat = np.einsum('ipxy,pxy->ixy', B, h)
g = np.fft.ifft2(g_hat, axes=(-2, -1)).real
mu_el = g[0] / Nv
eta_derivative_el = 2 * eta * g[1:] / Nv
# Elastic energy, with NumPy's default FFT normalization:
N = theta.shape[-2] * theta.shape[-1]
Fe = 0.5 * dx * dy / N * np.sum((h.conj() * g_hat).real)
Julia: the same kernel contraction and chain rule
# theta: (nx,ny,4); B: (nx,ny,4,4); eta: (nx,ny,3)
# theta[:,:,1]=c; theta[:,:,2:4]=eta.^2
h = fft(theta, (1, 2))
g_hat = zeros(ComplexF64, size(h))
for i in 1:4, p in 1:4
    g_hat[:,:,i] .+= B[:,:,i,p] .* h[:,:,p]
end
g = real.(ifft(g_hat, (1, 2)))
mu_el = g[:,:,1] ./ Nv
eta_derivative_el = 2 .* eta .* g[:,:,2:4] ./ Nv
N = size(theta,1) * size(theta,2)
Fe = 0.5 * dx * dy / N * sum(real.(conj.(h) .* g_hat))

Check the derivative against the energy.

For a small perturbation hᵢ(r), the central difference [Fₑ(θ + ah) − Fₑ(θ − ah)]/(2a) should approach ∫ ∑ᵢ gᵢhᵢ dV. To check the η derivative, perturb η and rebuild θᵢ = ηᵢ² before evaluating each energy. This catches a missing factor of two, an FFT normalization error, or an incorrect field-index contraction.

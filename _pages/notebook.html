A MICROSTRUCTURE, A WORD, A QUESTION

It looks like a labyrinth.
Is it really bicontinuous?

Books often describe spinodal microstructures as “bicontinuous.” At an average composition of 0.5, the picture may look convincing. But at 0.4, where is the bicontinuity? And even at 0.5, what exactly have we established by looking at a two-dimensional image?

These are computed teaching microstructures, not experimental photographs: 256 × 256 periodic grids, the same initial perturbation (seed 20260921), dimensionless time 400, Δt = 0.2, Δx = 1, and no elastic contribution. Fixed rainbow scale: c = 0 blue, 0.5 green, 1 red. The chemical free-energy density is \(f(c)=A c^2(1-c)^2\), with \(A=1\) and \(M=\kappa=1\). Its bulk coexistence compositions are 0 and 1.

First, what do we mean by “continuous”?

A useful operational definition is that each phase contains a connected network extending across the material over the directions and length scales of interest. Some definitions demand that each entire phase be connected; others allow isolated pockets provided each phase has a spanning network. We should state which meaning we use. A winding stripe, a branching network, and disconnected curved islands are different structures even when all look labyrinthine.

{% include turtle-maze.html %}

Two dimensions are not three dimensions.

In a plane, one path cannot pass over another without crossing it. Two phases can both extend along parallel stripes, but that does not establish a connected network in every direction. A 2D slice can also cut a connected 3D phase into apparently separate islands. Conversely, a genuinely 2D simulation should not be interpreted as evidence for a 3D interconnected structure.

Periodic boundaries matter too: opposite edges of these images are identified. Connectivity must be assessed on that periodic domain. Touching an image edge is not by itself proof that a phase wraps around the domain.

Measure it instead of naming it from appearance.

For this pair, we split the diffuse composition field at a threshold and count connected components using edge-sharing pixels (four-neighbor connectivity). We also track noncontractible loops: a component “wraps x” or “wraps y” when it contains a path around the periodic domain in that direction. A connection only at a pixel corner is excluded. This is a stated digital convention, not a threshold-independent verdict about continuum morphology.

Composition and phase fraction: the lever rule.

For bulk phases at \(c_0=0\) and \(c_1=1\), the lever rule gives

\[\bar c=\phi_0c_0+\phi_1c_1,\qquad \phi_1=\bar c,\qquad \phi_0=1-\bar c.\]

Thus the ideal phase fractions are 50% high-c / 50% low-c at mean composition 0.5, and 40% high-c / 60% low-c at mean composition 0.4. Here composition is scaled between the two bulk minima, and fractions refer to area in this two-dimensional model.

The ideal bulk phase fraction below follows this lever rule exactly. The measured region area counts pixels above or below the selected threshold in the actual diffuse field. At finite time, interfaces and departures from the bulk endpoint compositions make those two quantities differ. Changing the threshold changes the geometric measurement, not the conserved mean or the ideal lever-rule fractions. “Largest component” is expressed as a percentage of the measured region area.

Do we need differential geometry and topology?

Topology answers the connectivity question: components, loops, holes, and percolating paths. Differential geometry describes the shape of an interface, including its length, area and curvature, and helps explain how it evolves. They complement each other. We do not need a full course in either subject to begin: labeling connected components and testing spanning or periodic wrapping is already much stronger than judging a picture.

Betti numbers count components and independent loops; Euler characteristic combines such information. But a single Euler characteristic does not uniquely specify a network or prove percolation. Its interpretation also depends on boundary conditions.

The discussion I want to have: when we say “bicontinuous,” which phase definition, connectivity rule, boundary condition, direction and observation scale are we assuming? Composition 0.5 does not certify it, and composition 0.4 does not universally rule it out. One simulation, seed and time is an example, not a universal threshold.

THE PEOPLE BEHIND THE CURIOSITY

How SHAP sparked
my interest in AI/ML.

As I explored with my wife, Joyita, I found myself becoming curious about ideas I had not spent much time with before. She would experiment with machine learning while I stuck to phase-field modeling. She was using SHAP to understand a thermoelectrics database as part of her PhD research. When she showed me what it could reveal, it caught my imagination.

That moment sparked my interest in AI and machine learning. I owe this learning to her: to her experiments, our conversations, and the curiosity she shared with me. Sometimes the inspiration for a new research direction begins with someone beside us saying, “Look at this.”

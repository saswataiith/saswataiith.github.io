START WITH ESHELBY AND MURA

Cut. Transform. Strain to fit. Weld. Relax.

The physical starting point is the inclusion thought experiment used in micromechanics. Imagine cutting a region out of an unstressed matrix. Let that free region undergo its stress-free transformation strain. Apply tractions to restore the shape needed to fit the original hole, place it back, weld the coherent interface, and finally allow the entire body to relax elastically.

  1. Cut: remove a region having the intended inclusion shape.
  2. Transform: impose its stress-free transformation strain, or eigenstrain, while it is unconstrained.
  3. Strain to fit: apply auxiliary tractions until it recovers the shape of the original cavity.
  4. Weld: join it coherently to the surrounding matrix.
  5. Relax: solve mechanical equilibrium for the inclusion and matrix together.

This construction separates transformation strain from elastic strain. Eshelby showed how an ellipsoidal inclusion behaves inside an elastic medium. Mura developed eigenstrain as a general language for defects and inclusions. Khachaturyan then expressed arbitrary populations of coherent transformed regions through shape or phase fields and solved their collective elastic interactions in Fourier space.

Foundational sources: J. D. Eshelby, “The Determination of the Elastic Field of an Ellipsoidal Inclusion, and Related Problems,” Proceedings of the Royal Society A 241 (1957), 376–396; T. Mura, Micromechanics of Defects in Solids, 2nd ed. (1987); A. G. Khachaturyan, Theory of Structural Transformations in Solids, Chapter 7.

A CONTINUING RESEARCH PROGRAMME

One framework, many materials and mechanisms.

Elastic stress effects run through our work on multivariant transformations, phase separation in ternary alloys, coherent precipitates and Ni-base superalloys, ferroic domains, grain-boundary migration and deformation in heterogeneous materials. The models range from homogeneous microelasticity to elastically anisotropic and inhomogeneous systems, and from phase-field evolution to FFT-based elasticity, dislocation dynamics and crystal plasticity.

The formulation below introduces the common physical chain. A local stress-free transformation strain creates elastic strain when compatibility and boundary conditions prevent unconstrained relaxation. Mechanical equilibrium determines the relaxed field, and its elastic energy alters the thermodynamic driving force for microstructural evolution.

Fields define the local stress-free strain.

A model may use composition, structural variants, polarization, orientation, plastic strain or other fields. Their local values determine the eigenstrain, which is the strain the material would adopt if it could transform without constraint. For one composition field and three structural variants, a useful specialization is

\[\begin{aligned}\epsilon^0_{ij}(\mathbf r)&=\sum_{p=0}^{3}\theta_p(\mathbf r)\epsilon^{(p)}_{ij}\\ \theta_0&=c,\qquad \theta_i=\eta_i^2\quad(i=1,2,3).\end{aligned}\]

This four-field expression illustrates one multivariant model. Other applications use the same principle with a different field-to-eigenstrain map. Squaring a structural order parameter makes its eigenstrain contribution unchanged when its sign reverses.

Constraint turns transformation strain into stress.

A coherent microstructure cannot generally realize every local eigenstrain independently. Elastic strain is the difference between compatible total strain and eigenstrain. The constitutive law gives stress, and mechanical equilibrium determines the displacement field.

\[\begin{aligned}\epsilon^{\mathrm{el}}_{ij}&=\epsilon_{ij}-\epsilon^0_{ij}\\\sigma^{\mathrm{el}}_{ij}&=C_{ijkl}\epsilon^{\mathrm{el}}_{kl}\\\frac{\partial\sigma^{\mathrm{el}}_{ij}}{\partial r_j}&=0\\F_e&=\frac12\int_V\sigma^{\mathrm{el}}_{ij}\epsilon^{\mathrm{el}}_{ij}\,dV.\end{aligned}\]

Repeated spatial indices are summed. Eigenstrain and elastic strain are distinct: eigenstrain describes the local stress-free transformation, while elastic strain is the constrained response that produces stress.

The assumptions define the solver.

The closed-form Fourier kernel introduced in the linked derivation assumes linear elasticity, homogeneous stiffness, coherent interfaces, periodic fields and zero applied mean stress. It is a foundation, not a universal restriction. Our broader formulations treat elastic anisotropy, spatially varying stiffness, polycrystals, applied loading and coupling with plastic deformation. Direct iterative and equivalent-eigenstrain solvers are used when elastic inhomogeneity makes the homogeneous kernel insufficient.

Elasticity feeds back into evolution.

The total free energy combines chemical, interfacial and elastic contributions. Elastic energy therefore enters the variational driving forces for conserved composition fields and nonconserved structural fields. Mechanical equilibrium is solved at each required stage so that the evolving microstructure and mechanical fields remain coupled.

\[F=F_{\mathrm{chem}}+F_{\mathrm{grad}}+F_{\mathrm{el}}+\cdots\]

This chain connects transformation strains with morphology, orientation, interactions, coarsening, splitting, rafting and variant selection. It also makes clear where model assumptions enter and which solver is valid for a given material system.

This teaching presentation follows a connected micromechanical lineage. Eshelby’s inclusion construction and Mura’s eigenstrain method establish the physical problem. Khachaturyan’s Chapter 7 extends it to arbitrary coherent multiphase microstructures, homogeneous-modulus elastic energy and inclusion interactions. Our Communications in Computational Physics paper develops a spectral iterative perturbation method for spatially varying stiffness and effective properties. My thesis appendices provide one detailed multivariant derivation; the wider research programme includes both homogeneous and inhomogeneous formulations.

Why can the red turtle become trapped?

Periodicity identifies opposite boundaries: leaving one edge returns to the corresponding point on the other. Connectivity still requires every step, including a boundary step, to stay in the selected phase. Periodicity does not join disconnected islands. In this saved 2D field, the red phase has 16 components and no periodic wrapping loop; the largest blue component wraps in both directions. Four edge-sharing neighbors are used. Allowing diagonal contacts does not restore red wrapping.

Mean composition c̄ = 0.5 is a global conservation condition. Threshold c = 0.5 is the separate rule assigning pixels to red or blue. Equal mean composition guarantees neither equal connectedness nor exactly equal thresholded areas: here red occupies about 49.86%.

The symmetric free energy f(c) = f(1−c) makes a solution and its phase-swapped counterpart equally admissible. This symmetry belongs to the equations and statistical ensemble; the two phases need not have identical topology in each finite realization.

Two disjoint networks cannot cross in a plane. Both phases can nevertheless form parallel spanning bands, so bicontinuity is possible in 2D. Three dimensions permit much richer interpenetrating networks. The example below demonstrates measured bicontinuity in one specific simulated 3D field.

These connectivity calculations establish the topology of the saved discrete fields. They do not establish numerical convergence or show that either topology is representative of symmetric spinodal decomposition. Grid refinement, timestep reduction, multiple seeds and different coarsening times are needed to investigate robustness; those studies have not been completed here.

In that spirit, here is my face evolving under two different equations. Inspired by Prof. W. Craig Carter, MIT. His historical homepage shows image evolution under Cahn–Hilliard and Allen–Cahn dynamics.

My portrait is the energy minimum.

My portrait is first broken up by shuffling its pixels, producing a distorted, noise-like image. Now define an energy whose minimum is the intact portrait. Both equations move downhill in that energy, and the face emerges as the end product of forward evolution.

Left: Allen–Cahn (nonconserved), \(L=0.03\). Right: Cahn–Hilliard (conserved), \(M=10^{-5}\). Values are dimensionless.

The apparent noise is my color portrait broken up into shuffled pixels. I shuffle whole RGB pixels, so their original colors remain together. As the fields evolve toward the energy minimum, the face returns. The preview shows the final computed frame; press Play to start from the shuffled image.

How do we make the color portrait the minimum?

I represent the red, green and blue channels by three independent fields. Let \(r_j(x,y)\) be a channel of my fixed portrait and \(u_j(x,y,t)\) the corresponding evolving field. The index \(j\) identifies red, green or blue; \(x,y\) are position coordinates and \(t\) is time. I map each channel's 0–255 values to −1–1. All quantities in this example are dimensionless.

\[F[\mathbf u]=\frac12\int_\Omega\sum_{j=R,G,B}(u_j-r_j)^2\,dA,\qquad\mu_j=\frac{\delta F}{\delta u_j}=u_j-r_j.\]

Here \(F\) is the total image energy, \(\Omega\) is the image domain, \(dA\) is an area element, and \(\mu_j\) is the energy derivative for channel \(j\). Every squared difference is nonnegative. The unique unconstrained minimum occurs when every channel matches the portrait: \(\mathbf u=\mathbf r\), where \(F=0\). The symbol on the right is the number zero. This deliberately designed image energy is different from the usual double-well free energy of a material.

\[\partial_tu_j=-L(u_j-r_j)\quad\text{(Allen–Cahn)},\qquad\partial_tu_j=M\nabla^2(u_j-r_j)\quad\text{(Cahn–Hilliard)}.\]

\(L=0.03\) sets the Allen–Cahn relaxation rate; \(M=10^{-5}\) is the Cahn–Hilliard mobility. The Laplacian \(\nabla^2\) adds the second spatial derivatives in \(x\) and \(y\). With this local quadratic energy, the Cahn–Hilliard conservation law gives a second-order equation. The familiar fourth-order term would require a gradient-energy contribution, which this restoration example does not include.

With periodic boundaries, both energies decrease:

\[\frac{dF}{dt}\bigg|_{\mathrm{AC}}=-L\int_\Omega\sum_j\mu_j^2\,dA\le0,\qquad\frac{dF}{dt}\bigg|_{\mathrm{CH}}=-M\int_\Omega\sum_j|\nabla\mu_j|^2\,dA\le0.\]

Cahn–Hilliard preserves the mean of each channel for any initial field. Allen–Cahn does not impose that conservation law. Its channel means approach the target means. Here shuffling preserves all three target means, so the Allen–Cahn means stay fixed as a special case. That does not make Allen–Cahn conserved.

If a starting channel mean differed from its target mean, Cahn–Hilliard would approach the target channel plus a constant offset. The offset equals the initial mean minus the target mean. Allen–Cahn would remove that offset.

I solve the linear equations with exact propagators on a 256 × 256 periodic grid of width 32. The random seed is 19. Frames use nonuniform simulation times, so playback does not compare physical rates. The final frame is calculated at time 30,000,000, rather than replaced by the portrait. The displayed energy is \(F/(3|\Omega|)\), the total energy divided by the domain area \(|\Omega|\) and three channels. Both solutions approach the target asymptotically; a computed zero can occur at finite floating-point precision. Small computed values are shown in scientific notation. Numerical fields are not clipped; clipping is used only to display RGB values.

Can the Allen–Cahn image drift?

Mean drift and motion of the image are different questions. For this energy, the exact Allen–Cahn solution is

\[u_j(x,y,t)=r_j(x,y)+\big[u_j(x,y,0)-r_j(x,y)\big]e^{-Lt}.\]

Each pixel relaxes toward its fixed target value. This can look like a gradual reveal of the face, but it does not translate the face across the image. A translated portrait is not an energy minimum because the target is tied to fixed positions.

The channel mean obeys the same relaxation rule: \(\overline u_j(t)-\overline r_j=[\overline u_j(0)-\overline r_j]e^{-Lt}\), where the overbar denotes a spatial mean. Thus Allen–Cahn can change the mean when the initial mean differs from the target mean. The shuffled starting image has no such difference.

Other Allen–Cahn models can move interfaces when their free energy supplies a driving force. Motion is possible in those models; the fixed-target energy used here does not demonstrate it.

The number shown under each panel is its dimensionless mean energy density: that panel’s total energy \(F\), divided by the image area and by the three RGB channels. The two energies and what is conserved

We treat the red, green and blue channels as three independent signed fields. This is an image analogy, not a coupled material or perceptual color model.

\[F_{\rm portrait}=\frac12\int\sum_j(u_j-r_j)^2\,dA,\qquad F_{\rm domains}=\int\sum_j\left[\frac{(u_j^2-1)^2}{4}+\frac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla u_j|^2\right]dA.\]
\[\partial_tu_j=M\nabla^2\frac{\delta F}{\delta u_j}.\]

On the right, the portrait supplies the initial field, which evolves under a double-well and gradient energy. On the left, the portrait is explicitly encoded as the energy minimum. Both runs preserve each channel’s mean on the periodic domain. The same zero-mean noise is added to each starting portrait, so the left-hand target is compatible with conservation.

Grid: 256 × 312, spacing 1, M = 1, κ = 0.4. The right-hand nonlinear evolution uses a stabilized semi-implicit Fourier step with Δt = 0.1 and stabilization 2; the left uses the exact linear Fourier propagator. The displayed quantity is \(F/(3|\Omega|)\), a dimensionless mean energy density. The two values belong to different functionals, so their absolute magnitudes should not be compared.
